How Many Nails Per Shingle Should Your Roofing Bid Specify?

Otter Quotes · Updated November 2026

← All Guides

Your roofing bid should name the fastener, the number per shingle and the instructions the crew will follow. The Asphalt Roofing Manufacturers Association (ARMA) says most asphalt shingles need a minimum of four roofing nails per full-size strip shingle, and that some shingles and conditions call for a different number. Ask each bidder to write theirs down.

Why does nailing belong in a roofing bid?

Because nails are what hold the shingles to the roof, and a bid that is silent on them leaves the choice to the crew on the day. ARMA says nails that are improperly located or driven can lead to sealing failures, blow-offs, raised tabs and buckling.

You also cannot easily check the nailing after the shingles are on. ARMA says nails should never be placed where they will be fully or partially visible after the roof is complete, so the practical moment to learn what a contractor plans is before you sign. Two bids with the same shingle and the same total can describe very different fastening. Writing the answer into the bid costs a contractor one sentence and gives you something to compare.

How many nails per shingle does a roof need?

At least four on most asphalt shingles, according to ARMA, and the shingle maker's instructions decide the rest. ARMA says that for most asphalt shingles a minimum of four nails is required per full-size strip shingle, and that for some shingles and application circumstances the required number may be different. It tells installers to follow the manufacturer's instructions.

ARMA also says the International Building Code and International Residential Code require roofing nails to fasten asphalt shingles, and that installers should consider those codes together with the manufacturer's instructions. ARMA's bulletin notes that local, state and federal building officials have jurisdiction in your area, so ask the contractor which code the crew is following and which shingle instructions it is reading from.

Are nails or staples better for roof shingles?

Nails. ARMA says the IBC and IRC building codes require roofing nails to fasten asphalt shingles, and GAF, a shingle manufacturer, says staples are not recommended by GAF and most industry organizations. If a crew uses staples on GAF shingles, GAF says the shingle warranty stays in effect, but problems resulting from staples, including wind blow-off, are not covered.

The same bulletin says that UL, the testing organization, has begun doing its higher wind speed testing only with nails, and that many current UL wind listings are based on nails. If a bid says only "fastened per manufacturer," ask what the fastener is. Where a contractor says staples are acceptable, GAF's bulletin describes them as at least 16-gauge galvanized steel with a crown of at least 15/16 inch, so ask which staple and why.

What size and type of nail should a bid name?

A corrosion-resistant roofing nail of the right gauge, head size and length. ARMA says nails must have a nominal shank diameter of at least 12 gauge (0.105 inch) and a head diameter of at least 3/8 inch, and must be galvanized steel, stainless steel, aluminum or copper.

Length matters too. ARMA says to choose nails long enough to penetrate at least 3/4 inch into the roof deck, or, where the deck is thinner than 3/4 inch, through the sheathing by at least 1/8 inch. It says to account for the number of shingle layers, the shingle thickness, underlayment, ridge caps and flashing. A bid does not need to list the gauge of every nail, but a contractor should be able to tell you what they plan to use.

Where on the shingle do the nails go?

Below the sealant strip and above the part that will show. For most shingles with sealant on the top surface, ARMA says to place nails below the sealant strip but above the area that will be visible when the roof is done. Shingles with sealant on the back often have lines that show where the nails go.

ARMA lists more placement rules. No nail head should be less than 1 inch from either end of the shingle. Nails should not go into knot holes, cracks or spaces in the deck. For multi-layer laminated shingles, a manufacturer may require nails to pass through every layer. You do not need to supervise this. You can ask each contractor how the crew is trained to follow the manufacturer's nail line.

Does the nail gun matter?

Yes. ARMA says nails may be driven by hand or with a pneumatic tool that has been properly adjusted, and that failure to use a properly adjusted air system can lead to sealing failures, raised tabs, distortions and blow-offs. Nails should not be underdriven, overdriven or driven crookedly.

ARMA says incorrectly applied nails should be repaired immediately. Underdriven nails can be tapped down. Overdriven or crooked nails should be removed, the hole repaired with asphalt roof cement that meets ASTM D4586, and another nail placed nearby. If that is not practical, ARMA says to replace the shingle. Ask each bidder who checks the work and whether they will fix a bad nail the same day.

Do nails affect the shingle warranty?

They can, and each manufacturer sets its own terms. GAF says staple problems are not covered by its shingle warranty. More broadly, ARMA says to follow the shingle manufacturer's instructions, and NRCA, the National Roofing Contractors Association, advises homeowners to read the warranty carefully and understand the provisions that will void it.

Read the warranty for the shingle your bid names and look for the installation requirements. Some brands tie their higher wind levels to a special installation, and our guide to roof wind ratings explains how that works. Our guide to manufacturer-backed roof warranties shows what else to look for.

What to ask every roofing contractor about nailing

Put the same questions to every bidder and keep the answers in writing, so you can set them side by side before you compare totals. NRCA advises homeowners to insist on a written proposal with complete descriptions of the work and specifications, which is where these answers belong.

Copy this list into a note and send it to each contractor before you compare totals.

  • Will you use roofing nails or staples, and what fastener is written in the bid?
  • How many nails per shingle will you use, and what does the manufacturer's instruction for my shingle say?
  • Are the nails galvanized, stainless, aluminum or copper?
  • Are the nails long enough to reach into the deck?
  • Will the crew nail by hand or with a pneumatic nail gun, and who sets and checks the gun?
  • How will the crew follow the manufacturer's nail line, and who checks it?
  • If a nail is overdriven or crooked, how is it fixed?
  • How are the starter strip and the ridge caps fastened?
  • Does the manufacturer's warranty for my shingle say anything about the fasteners?
  • Will you put the fastener and the nail count in the written proposal?

Frequently asked questions

How many nails should be in each roof shingle?

ARMA says most asphalt shingles need a minimum of four roofing nails per full-size strip shingle, and that some shingles and application circumstances call for a different number. The shingle manufacturer's instructions decide, so ask each contractor to write down the count and the instruction they are following.

Are staples allowed on shingles?

GAF says it does not recommend staples. It says its shingle warranty stays in effect if staples are used, but problems resulting from staples, including wind blow-off, are not covered. Other brands set their own terms. Ask the contractor to put the fastener in writing and read the warranty for your shingle.

What kind of roofing nail should be used?

ARMA says roofing nails must be galvanized steel, stainless steel, aluminum or copper, with a shank of at least 12 gauge and a head of at least 3/8 inch. ARMA says they should be long enough to go at least 3/4 inch into the deck, or through thinner sheathing by at least 1/8 inch. Ask which nail the contractor will use.

Where should the nails go on the shingle?

ARMA says to place nails below the sealant strip and above the area that will show, no closer than 1 inch from either end of the shingle, and not into knot holes or gaps in the deck. Shingles with sealant on the back often have a printed line. Ask how the crew follows the manufacturer's nail line.

Can I check the nailing after the roof is finished?

Not easily. ARMA says nails should never be placed where they will be fully or partially visible after the roof is complete, so the nailing is hidden once the job is done. That is why the questions belong before you sign. Ask each contractor to write the fastener and nail count into the proposal, and ask who checks the work during the job.
