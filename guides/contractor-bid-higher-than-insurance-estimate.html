My Contractor's Bid Is Higher Than the Insurance Estimate. What Now?

Otter Quotes · Updated October 2026

← All Guides

A bid that comes in higher than the insurance estimate is usually not a red flag. Your adjuster and your contractor do different jobs, and a bid that lists work the estimate does not is often your contractor doing theirs. Insurers see these requests every day, and they are called supplements. What you need from your contractor is a clear answer on how supplements will be handled and who pays if an item is not covered. Get that answer in writing before you sign.

Your contractor and your adjuster have different jobs

The adjuster's job is to see what the storm damaged and start a record of the items that were damaged and need to be repaired. That record is the estimate. It is a starting point for your claim.

The contractor's job is to build the roof. That means pricing every piece of work it takes to put a finished, code-compliant roof on your house, and standing behind the result. Two people with two different jobs will write two different lists.

Your contractor is your expert on code, material and installation

The adjuster is not on your roof tearing off shingles. Your contractor is the one who knows what the local code requires, which materials the manufacturer's installation instructions call for, and what a proper installation takes on your roof. When your contractor reviews the adjuster's estimate, they should find items that were left off. That is part of the job.

On a typical roof, items that turn up this way include flashing, drip edge, an ice barrier at the eaves, starter strips and ridge cap, and the permit and disposal. Which of these apply depends on your roof, your local code and the manufacturer's instructions. A contractor who reads the estimate line by line and tells you what is missing is doing what you hired them to do.

This is the process working as it should

A contractor asking the insurer to add items to an estimate is a supplement request. Insurance companies process supplement requests every day. The work of finding the missing items, documenting them and sending them to the insurer is a job in itself, and whole companies exist to help contractors with it, like our good friends Troy and Josh over at Balance Claims. Balance Claims describes its work as handling "File reviews, Estimating, code research, and submitting and following up on supplements" for restoration contractors.

Otter Quotes has no business relationship with Balance Claims; the founders are longtime friends of ours. You do not need to deal with a supplement company yourself. Your contractor decides who, if anyone, helps with theirs.

A supplement is a request. Whether your insurer approves it, in whole or in part, is up to your insurer and your policy. Neither your contractor nor Otter Quotes can know the answer in advance.

What to ask your contractor: how will supplements be handled?

What you need to know from your contractor is how supplements will be handled, and what happens to the price if the insurer does not pay for an item. Ask these before you sign:

  1. Who requests supplements, and when? Does your contractor handle the request, or do you?
  2. If a line item is not covered, who is responsible for the cost? Ask for a direct answer: you, the contractor, or a split.
  3. What is the price if the insurer approves none of it, some of it, or all of it?
  4. How will you be told, and in what form, about each change to the price?

What is a code upgrade?

A code upgrade is a supplement for something that was not on your roof when it was damaged, but that the current building code requires when the new roof goes on. Codes change over time, and when a permit is pulled for a repair or replacement, the work is expected to meet the code in force today. Drip edge and an ice barrier along the eaves are examples of items some local codes require on a new asphalt shingle roof, and an older roof may not have had them. Check with your contractor and your local building department for what applies where you live.

Some policies cover these upgrades. Some do not. In policy language, this coverage is often called "ordinance or law" coverage. It may be included in the policy, added by endorsement or excluded, and where it is included it is often capped at a percentage of your dwelling coverage limit. Look for it on your declarations page, and ask your agent or insurer how yours reads.

Then ask your contractor: if my policy does not pay for code upgrades, who is responsible for that expense?

If a contractor tells you that you will not be responsible for these costs, get it in writing before you sign the agreement. Ask them to point out the provisions in the contract that say you will not be responsible for these expenses. If it is not in the contract, you may not be able to rely on it.

Your contract is between you and the contractor. How any insurance payment relates to the contract price is a question for your insurer and your policy, and Otter Quotes cannot answer it. Read every page of the contract, and ask what any sentence about your insurance claim means before you sign. Before you sign, also ask the contractor to show in writing what you owe, when each payment is due and what each payment covers.

Why can a contractor's bid be higher than the insurance estimate?

The estimate is your insurer's document for your claim, and the bid is a contractor's offer to do the work. Each lists work, quantities, materials and prices, and a different person wrote each. A higher total can come from items left off the estimate, a different material, a different quantity, a different price for the same item, or a mix.

A difference is a question to ask, and the table below is how to ask it. If the words on the estimate are unfamiliar, our explainer on what "scope of loss" means covers the terms.

How do I compare the bid and the estimate side by side?

Put the two documents in one table with a row for each item, so every difference sits where you can see it. Then ask the contractor to explain every row that differs, in writing.

  1. Get the whole estimate. If you do not have every page, ask your insurer for a full copy.
  2. Ask the contractor for a line-item bid. A single lump sum hides what is and is not included.
  3. Make a row for each item. Add a row for every item either document lists, such as removal of the old material, the main material, small parts and trim, permit, disposal and cleanup.
  4. Fill in both columns. Copy what the estimate says and what the bid says for each item: quantity, unit, material and price.
  5. Mark each row. Write Same, Different, On the estimate only, or On the bid only.
  6. Send the questions. Ask the contractor to explain every row that is not Same, in writing.

Copy this table into a document or spreadsheet. Leave the two middle columns blank until you have both documents in front of you.

Item What the estimate lists What the bid lists Question to ask the contractor
Removal of the old material     Does the bid include removal, and how many layers?
Main material     Which manufacturer, product line and color?
Quantity and unit     Where did your measurements come from?
Small parts and trim     Which are listed, and which are new or reused?
Items required by code     Which items does the code require, and who pays if my policy does not?
Permit, disposal and cleanup     Who handles each, and are they in the total?
Warranty     What does it cover, for how long, and who stands behind it?
Start date and length of the job     What is written, and what could change it?

Some rows, such as warranty and start date, may be on the bid only. Mark them that way.

What should I ask the contractor about each difference?

Ask the contractor to say in writing why each line is in the bid and what it covers. Send every contractor the same questions, so you can compare the answers side by side.

  • Which lines in your bid are not on the insurer's estimate, and what does each one cover?
  • Which of those lines are required by code, and which are your recommendation?
  • Which lines on the estimate are not in your bid, and why?
  • Where did your quantities and measurements come from?
  • Does your bid name a product line where the estimate names only a category?
  • What does your total include: removal, disposal, permit and cleanup?
  • What could change the price after I sign, and how would a change be written down?
  • Can you put every answer in writing, with a date?

Who do I ask about the insurer's side?

Questions about what the estimate says, how your policy applies and what your insurer does with a supplement belong with your insurer, in writing where you can. Otter Quotes takes no part in that conversation. Keep a copy of every answer, and write down the date, the name of the person you spoke with and what they said. Questions you can ask:

  • Can you walk me through how this line on your estimate is described and measured?
  • If a contractor sends you a supplement request, who handles it and what do you need from the contractor?
  • Does my policy include ordinance or law (code upgrade) coverage, and what is the limit?
  • Where do I find the dates and deadlines that apply to my claim?

Does a higher bid mean something is wrong?

Not by itself. A higher bid is a reason to ask what is different, not a reason to drop the contractor or to choose the bid. The estimate is one thing to compare a bid with. Materials, warranty, schedule and who does the work belong in the comparison too.

With one bid, you hold one contractor's list and price against the estimate. With several bids on the same scope, you can compare side by side and see which differences show up in only one bid and which show up in more than one. Our roof bid worksheet lines bids up row by row.
