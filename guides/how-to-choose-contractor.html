How to Choose a Contractor for Insurance Repairs

What to check, what to avoid, and what to ask before you sign anything.

After your property is damaged — whether from a windstorm, hail, fire, or water — the pressure to get it fixed quickly is real. Contractors know this, and not all of them operate honestly. Choosing the wrong one can mean subpar repairs, out-of-pocket costs your insurer won't cover, legal problems from an assignment of benefits you didn't fully understand, or simply work that fails inspection.

This guide walks you through every step of selecting a qualified contractor for insurance repair work: what credentials to verify, what red flags to recognize before you're committed, which questions will reveal how a contractor actually operates, and how to read a contract before you sign it.

If you've already filed your claim, you may also find our guide on how to file a property damage insurance claim useful for understanding where contractor selection fits in the overall process.

1. What Credentials to Check

Before you spend any time talking scope or price with a contractor, confirm they have the credentials the job requires. Have the contractor email you the documents before you meet, then confirm each one is accurate before you sign a contract.

The pre-meeting email

Call the contractor's main office to set the appointment. Ask for the name, email address and phone number of the sales rep who will come to your home. Then email that rep directly and ask for the documents. The email can be as simple as this:

Dear [Name],

Before our meeting on [date], please send me the following so I can review them:

  1. A copy of every state and local license that applies to work at my address;
  2. A certificate of insurance showing your general liability and workers' compensation coverage, with me listed as the certificate holder;
  3. A copy of any bond you have filed with a state or local government agency; and
  4. A blank copy of your standard contract.

Thank you,
[Your name]

A professional, well-organized contractor can send these in minutes. A delay is a red flag. It may mean the contractor is new to the industry and hasn't been asked before, that they're disorganized and don't keep these documents on hand, or that they don't have them at all, possibly because of financial trouble.

This email alone can tell you more about the company and the sales rep than a meeting at your kitchen table ever will. If the rep can't get you standard documents quickly and easily now, things will only get harder as the job moves along.

What each document should show

1

Contractor's license

Licensing rules for roofing, general contracting, electrical, plumbing, HVAC and other trades differ from state to state, and in some places the city or county sets them. The license you receive should name the same business that appears on the estimate, and the license type should match your job. If the contractor says they're "exempt" from licensing, don't take their word for it. Confirm the exemption with the office that would issue the license (Section 2).

2

General liability insurance

This covers damage the contractor causes to your property while working. Without it, if a crew member drops a ladder through your skylight or causes a leak that ruins your flooring, you may be left trying to recover the cost from the contractor yourself. Look at the coverage limit on the certificate. For a large or complex project, it's reasonable to ask for higher limits.

3

Workers' compensation insurance

This covers the contractor's employees if they're hurt on the job. The rules on who must carry it differ by state. The certificate should show a separate workers' compensation policy, not just general liability. A contractor who says they don't need it (for example, a sole proprietor with no employees) should be able to show you why.

4

Bonds

Some states and cities require contractors to file a license or permit bond. If a bond applies, the copy you receive should name the contractor's business, the agency it was filed with, and the surety company that issued it.

5

Standard contract

Read it before the meeting, not at the kitchen table. Section 6 covers the terms to refuse.

6

Business registration

You don't need to ask for this one; you can look it up yourself (Section 2). If the contractor is an LLC or corporation, it should be active in your state's business registry. A sole proprietor may not appear there, so ask what name the business is licensed under.

Checklist before you sign:
  • License confirmed active with the office that issues it
  • Certificate of insurance confirmed with the insurer (general liability and workers' comp)
  • Any bond confirmed with the agency or surety
  • Business name the same on the license, the certificate, the estimate and the contract
  • A physical business address (not a PO box or personal address)

2. Confirming the Documents Are Real

Documents can be faked. A license can be expired, and a certificate of insurance can be forged or issued on a policy that has since been cancelled. Here are the quickest ways to confirm what the contractor sent you.

Confirm the license (about 2 minutes)

Go straight to the website of the office that issued it: your state's contractor licensing board, or your city or county building department. Don't rely on a search result that might lead to a third-party listing. Search by license number (most accurate) or business name, and check that:

  • The status is "active" or "current," not expired, suspended or revoked
  • The license type matches the trade
  • The name matches the estimate and contract
  • The license covers your city or county (some licenses are local, not statewide)

Confirm the insurance (about 5 minutes)

Call the insurance company named on the certificate. Use the number on the insurer's official website, not the one printed on the document. Ask them to confirm the policy is active for the contractor's business. This is the only way to know the certificate wasn't fabricated or issued on a policy that was later cancelled. While you're on the phone:

  • Check the expiration dates. The policy must be active the day work starts and throughout the job. If it expires mid-project, ask for a renewed certificate before work continues.
  • Check that workers' compensation shows a policy number, not "not applicable," unless you've confirmed with your state's workers' compensation agency that the contractor is exempt.

Confirm any bond (about 2 minutes)

Many licensing boards show the bond on the contractor's license record. If yours doesn't, call the surety company named on the bond, again using the number from its own website, and confirm the bond is active.

Confirm the business registration (about 1 minute)

Search your state's business registry (in many states the secretary of state runs it) for the business name and confirm it's active.

Watch out for: a contractor who says they'll "send it later," sends documents for a different business name, or pushes back when you say you'll verify them. Any resistance to standard verification is a meaningful signal.

3. Red Flags to Watch For

Unscrupulous contractors operate year-round and target all types of insurance repairs — not just storm damage. Here are the most common warning signs.

Stop immediately if a contractor:
  • Asks you to sign an Assignment of Benefits (AOB) — this hands them rights under your insurance policy (see below)
  • Offers to waive or absorb your deductible — in Texas, for example, it is illegal for a contractor or roofer to offer to waive a deductible on a property-insurance claim without the insurer's consent (Texas Department of Insurance; H.B. 2102, 86th Legislature); ask your insurer or your state's department of insurance how your state treats this
  • Demands a large upfront cash payment before any work begins
  • Pressures you to sign immediately or claims the offer expires today
  • Cannot provide a physical business address (PO Boxes don't count)
  • Refuses to pull required permits or tells you permits aren't necessary
  • Quotes by the job only, with no itemized breakdown of labor and materials

Assignment of Benefits

AOB arrangements are worth understanding in detail because they are presented by some contractors as a convenience — "so you don't have to deal with the insurer." In practice, signing an AOB hands the contractor rights under your insurance policy. If you are handed one, do not sign it on the spot: read it closely and ask the contractor exactly what it lets them do.

Whether an AOB is allowed depends on your state and your policy. For example, Florida law says a policyholder may not assign post-loss benefits under a residential property insurance policy issued on or after January 1, 2023, with limited exceptions, and that an attempt to do so is void (Florida Statutes section 627.7152(13)). Wherever you live, consult with your insurer and possibly an attorney before signing any AOB.

Deductible Waivers

Your deductible is the part of a covered loss that your policy leaves you to pay. The Texas Department of Insurance calls an offer to waive or absorb it a selling point that bad contractors often use: "The contractor then cuts corners, uses lower quality products, or inflates the bill sent to the insurance company to cover the difference." In Texas it is illegal for a contractor or roofer to offer to waive a deductible on a property-insurance claim without the insurer's consent (Texas Department of Insurance; H.B. 2102, 86th Legislature). Ask your insurer or your state's department of insurance how your state treats a waived deductible.

Permit Avoidance

Ask whether the job needs a permit and who pulls it; your city or county building department can tell you. Permits mean the work is inspected to code. A contractor who won't pull a permit the job needs may be unlicensed, cutting scope on the work, or avoiding an inspection. Unpermitted work can complicate future sales of your home.

4. Questions to Ask Before You Hire

A well-qualified contractor will answer these questions readily and specifically. Evasiveness or impatience with credential questions is itself meaningful information.

1

"Can I have your license number and the name of your insurer?"

This is the first filter. Any legitimate contractor will hand these over without hesitation. A contractor who hedges, provides a license number for a different jurisdiction, or says they're "in the process of renewing" is telling you something important.

2

"Who will actually be doing the work — your employees or subcontractors?"

Many general contractors use subcontractors, which is normal. But if the job is subcontracted, confirm that the subcontractors are also licensed and insured. Ask for their license and COI as well, or ask the GC to confirm in writing that all subs are covered under the GC's policy.

3

"Have you done similar work in this area recently? Can you provide local references?"

Ask for three local references from comparable jobs — similar scope, similar construction type — completed within the last 12 months. Call the references. Ask them: Did the project finish on schedule? Did final costs match the estimate? Were there any inspection issues? Would you hire them again?

4

"Who pulls the permits, and will I receive copies of the inspection sign-offs?"

The contractor should pull the permits — not you. And you should receive paper or digital copies of all passed inspections before making final payment. This protects you if issues arise later.

5

"What is your payment schedule, and do you require a deposit?"

Ask for the deposit amount and the payment schedule in writing in the contract, with progress payments tied to completion milestones, and a final payment held until work is complete and passes inspection. Be wary of contractors who demand a large share of the price upfront, or who want full payment before work begins.

6

"What warranty do you provide on your workmanship, and do materials carry a manufacturer's warranty?"

Workmanship warranty lengths differ by contractor and trade. Get the warranty terms in writing in the contract — not just in the sales conversation. Understand the difference between the contractor's workmanship warranty and the manufacturer's material warranty, and confirm both are transferable if you sell the property.

7

"Have you worked on insurance repair jobs before, and are you familiar with my insurer's scope-of-loss format?"

Experience with insurance repair work specifically matters. Contractors who routinely work with insurance claims understand how to document scope, communicate with adjusters, and handle supplement requests if hidden damage is found once work begins.

5. Getting and Comparing Quotes

Never accept a single quote for insurance repair work. Multiple bids give you pricing context, reveal scope discrepancies, and leave you with a written record of what each contractor quoted.

Get At Least Three Written Estimates

All estimates should be in writing and should itemize: materials (type, grade, quantity), labor, disposal/haul-away, permit costs, and any subcontracted scope. A one-line estimate ("Roof replacement — $12,000") is not sufficient for insurance repair work. You need enough detail to compare bids against each other and against your adjuster's scope of loss.

Compare Against Your Adjuster's Estimate

Your insurance adjuster wrote a scope of loss — an itemized list of what they've determined needs repair and what they're willing to pay. Before hiring a contractor, review that document line by line against the contractor bids. Look for:

  • Scope gaps: Items a contractor bid includes that the adjuster's scope of loss does not — you may need to pay for these out of pocket, or ask your insurer about a supplement
  • Upgrade costs: Code upgrades or material substitutions not covered in the original scope — confirm who pays for these before work starts

Our guide on filing a property damage insurance claim walks through the adjuster estimate process in detail, including how to handle scope disputes before hiring a contractor.

On price and the lowest bid: The lowest bid is not automatically the best choice. If one bid is far below the others, ask that contractor what explains the difference in scope and materials. Your goal is fair market value for quality work that matches your insurer's scope — not the lowest number.

6. Reviewing the Contract Before You Sign

A contractor's proposal is not a contract until it's a signed, written agreement with specific terms. Here's what every insurance repair contract should contain — and what to refuse to sign.

What the Contract Must Include

  • Full legal name and license number of the contracting entity
  • Physical business address and contact information
  • Detailed scope of work — materials, grade, quantity, and labor tasks, not vague summaries
  • Estimated start date and substantial completion date, with provisions for delay
  • Payment schedule tied to project milestones, not arbitrary dates
  • Who is responsible for pulling permits
  • Workmanship warranty terms in writing
  • Dispute resolution process (arbitration clause terms, if any)
  • Change order procedure — any scope changes must be in writing before work proceeds

What to Refuse to Sign

Do not sign any document that:
  • Assigns your insurance benefits to the contractor (Assignment of Benefits / AOB)
  • Authorizes the contractor to negotiate your claim directly with your insurer without your involvement
  • Waives your right to dispute the final invoice
  • Includes a clause where you accept that permits "are not required" for the described scope — verify this independently
  • Has blank spaces in any material term — price, scope, dates

Change Orders

Hidden damage discovered once work begins is common in insurance repair jobs — water damage behind walls, rotten decking under shingles, deteriorated framing behind stucco. Legitimate contractors document discovered damage with photographs, provide a written change order describing the additional scope and cost, and wait for your signature before proceeding. Never allow a contractor to proceed with additional work based on a verbal agreement alone.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I verify a contractor's license?

If your state licenses the trade, its contractor licensing board website is the place to look. Search by business name or license number; if there is no state license, ask your city or county building department what applies. The license should be active, match the trade you need, and list the correct business entity. Don't rely on the contractor to provide a screenshot — look it up yourself on the issuing office's official website.

What insurance should a contractor carry?

At minimum, look for general liability insurance and workers' compensation. Request a Certificate of Insurance (COI) and call the insurance company directly to confirm the policy is active and hasn't been cancelled. The phone number should come from the insurer's official website, not the COI itself.

What is an Assignment of Benefits (AOB) and should I sign one?

An Assignment of Benefits (AOB) is a document that hands the contractor rights under your insurance policy. Whether an AOB is allowed, and what it lets the contractor do, depends on your state and your policy. Do not sign one on the spot: read it closely, ask the contractor exactly what it lets them do, and consult your insurer and, if needed, an attorney first.

How many quotes should I get for an insurance repair?

Get at least three written, itemized quotes. Multiple bids help you understand fair market pricing, identify unusually low bids, and give you leverage to negotiate scope gaps with your insurer. All bids should itemize materials, labor, and permit costs — not just a total job price.

Can my insurer tell me which contractor to use?

That depends on your policy and your state. Your insurer may have a preferred contractor network. Check your policy documents for any provisions about choosing a contractor, and ask your state's department of insurance if you are unsure of your options. If you choose your own contractor, ensure their bid aligns with your adjuster's scope before work begins to avoid payment disputes.

What are warning signs of a dishonest contractor?

Key red flags include: pressure to sign immediately, requests for large upfront cash payments, no physical business address, inability to provide verifiable credentials, asking you to sign an AOB, promising to waive or absorb your deductible, quoting a total price with no itemized breakdown, and unwillingness to pull permits. Any single one of these warrants caution; multiple red flags together should cause you to walk away.

What should I do if a contractor finds additional damage during the repair?

Ask the contractor to stop work, document the discovered damage with photographs, and contact your insurer before authorizing any additional scope. Ask your insurer how to submit a supplement request for the additional damage. Never authorize additional work based on a verbal agreement — require a written change order with documented scope and pricing before work resumes.
