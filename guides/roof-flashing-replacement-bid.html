Does Your Roof Replacement Bid Say What Happens to the Flashing?

Otter Quotes · Updated November 2026

← All Guides

Flashing is the metal that keeps water out where a roof meets a wall, a chimney or a pipe. A bid that does not say what happens to it leaves a leak-prone part of your roof to chance. Ask each contractor to write down which flashing is new, which is reused and who answers for a leak.

What is roof flashing, and why does it belong in a bid?

Flashing is thin metal, usually galvanized steel according to GAF, installed where a roof meets a vertical surface or where two roof sections meet. Its job is to steer water away from those joints. Shingles cover the open roof. Flashing covers the places shingles cannot.

Those joints are where a bid can stay quiet. GAF's technical bulletin calls poorly made or poorly installed flashings "the #1 cause of roof leaks." It also says proper flashing may be the single most important aspect of a new roof. That is one manufacturer's view of its own product category, but it is a good reason to ask every bidder for the same detail.

Which flashings should a replacement bid name?

A useful bid names each kind of flashing on your roof, not just "flashing included." Your roof may not have all of them, so ask each bidder to write down every flashing your roof has, whether it will be new or reused, and what material each new piece is made of.

  • Step flashing. Many small separate pieces of bent metal laid in a stair-step pattern where a roof meets a side wall or a chimney, each one overlapped by shingles.
  • Counterflashing. A second piece set above step or base flashing so water cannot run behind it. GAF's bulletin says all step flashing should be covered with an appropriate counterflashing.
  • Drip edge. Metal along the eaves and rakes that guides water toward the gutters. GAF's blog, citing ARMA, says it should be made of a corrosion-resistant material.
  • Valley flashing. Metal where two roof planes meet. An open valley shows the metal. A closed valley is covered by shingles.
  • Pipe and vent flashing. The collar or boot around plumbing stacks and other pipes that pass through the roof.
  • Chimney flashing. Usually a base piece plus counterflashing around the chimney.

A single long "L" strip of metal is not step flashing. GAF's flashing bulletin says "L" metal should not be used where a roof meets a side wall, because it tends to leak. Step flashing is the alternative it describes.

Will the contractor replace the old flashing or reuse it?

Ask, because the answer changes the work and the price. GAF's reuse bulletin says metal flashings may be reused in some cases, but GAF recommends replacing all of them whenever possible. Its flashing bulletin goes further for two spots: new step flashing should be used whenever possible, and through-roof penetrations such as plumbing stacks should have new flashings.

The reuse bulletin sets conditions. Drip edge, vent flashings, step flashing and counterflashing may be reused only if they are in like-new condition, with no excessive wear, corrosion, tearing or damage from tear-off. It says step flashing that does not match the size of the new shingles must be replaced whatever its condition, that existing plastic flashing is not acceptable, and it warns that metal flashing can be damaged during removal.

None of that tells you what your contractor will do. It tells you what questions are fair. A bid that says "reuse existing flashing" can be reasonable, but it should say which pieces, and who decides after the old roof is off that a piece is no longer like new.

Does the shingle warranty cover flashing?

Do not assume it does. GAF's flashing bulletin says its limited material warranty does not cover flashings. Its reuse bulletin says the GAF Shingle & Accessory Limited Warranty stays in effect even when existing materials are reused, but damage caused by reusing existing flashings is not covered.

The reuse bulletin adds that enhanced warranty coverage needs advance approval to reuse metal flashing. Other manufacturers word their warranties differently, and neither bulletin is your warranty. Read the warranty for the shingle your bid names. NRCA, the National Roofing Contractors Association, advises homeowners to read the warranty carefully and understand what will void it. Why can two bids differ on flashing?

Because flashing is easy to leave out of a summary and hard to see from the ground. One contractor may price new step flashing, new pipe flashings and new drip edge. Another may price only the shingles and treat the rest as "as needed." Both can write a clean one-line total.

That is why a lower number is not always a like-for-like number. Before you decide a bid is cheaper, check whether it covers the same flashing as the others. Is flashing a line in the bid, or a change order?

Ask before work starts. Some bids fold flashing into the roof price, and some list it separately. A contractor who finds flashing in poor shape once the old roof is off may propose extra work. A price in writing beforehand is far better than a surprise mid-job.

NRCA, the National Roofing Contractors Association, tells homeowners to "insist on a written proposal" that describes the work and specifications, the start and completion dates and how payment works. Ask each bidder how flashing is priced if more of it needs replacing than planned. What to ask every roofing contractor about flashing

Put the same questions to every bidder, and keep the answers in writing so you can set them side by side. Several of these follow what GAF's bulletins describe: new versus reused, step flashing, counterflashing, pipe flashings and old plastic pieces. Copy this list into a note and send it to each contractor before you compare totals.

  • Which flashings are on my roof today, and which will you replace?
  • Is the step flashing new, and is it covered by counterflashing?
  • Is the drip edge new, and does it run along the eaves and the rakes?
  • What will you use at each pipe and vent that comes through the roof?
  • What is the plan at the chimney and at any wall the roof meets?
  • If you plan to reuse any metal flashing, which pieces, and who decides they are like new?
  • Is there any plastic flashing, or one long "L" metal strip at a wall where step flashing belongs, and what replaces it?
  • Is flashing a line in the bid, or is it included in the roof price?
  • If more flashing needs replacing than planned, how is the extra work priced and agreed in writing?
  • Which warranty answers for a leak at the flashing, and what does it say about reused flashing?

Frequently asked questions

Why does a roof replacement bid need to mention flashing?

Flashing keeps water out where the roof meets walls, chimneys and pipes. GAF's technical bulletin calls poorly made or poorly installed flashings "the #1 cause of roof leaks." A bid that only says "flashing included" does not tell you which pieces are new, so ask each contractor to name them.

Should my contractor replace the old flashing with a new roof?

Ask. GAF says metal flashings may be reused in some cases but recommends replacing all of them whenever possible. It says new step flashing should be used whenever possible and through-roof pipe flashings should be new. Reuse is allowed only for like-new pieces. Have the contractor write down which pieces are new and which stay.

What is the difference between step flashing and counterflashing?

Step flashing is a series of small separate pieces of bent metal laid in a stair-step pattern where a roof meets a wall or chimney, overlapped by shingles. Counterflashing is a second piece set above it. GAF says all step flashing should be covered with an appropriate counterflashing. Ask your bid to name both where your roof has them.

Does the shingle warranty cover flashing?

Do not assume so. GAF's bulletins say its limited material warranty does not cover flashings, nor damage caused by reusing existing flashings. Other manufacturers may word this differently. Read the warranty for the shingle your bid names, and ask the contractor in writing who answers for a flashing leak.

Is new flashing a separate charge or part of the roof price?

It depends on the bid. Some contractors include flashing in the roof price and some list it separately. Ask each bidder which it is, and how extra flashing work is priced if it turns out to be needed. NRCA advises homeowners to "insist on a written proposal" that describes the work and specifications.
