The Contractor Workmanship Warranty: What It Covers and Why It Is Only as Good as the Company

Otter Quotes · Updated October 2026

← All Guides

A workmanship warranty is a contractor’s written promise about the installation, and it is only as strong as the company that signs it. This guide explains what it covers, how it differs from the manufacturer’s warranty, what to get in writing and what to ask every bidder. It states no outcome and promises nothing about what any contractor will offer.

What is a workmanship warranty, and how is it different from the manufacturer’s warranty?

A roof usually carries two promises from two different parties. A Hawaii state consumer agency, the Regulated Industries Complaints Office, puts it this way in its roofing brochure: “In roofing, there are usually two guarantees – the manufacturer’s and the contractor’s.” The manufacturer stands behind the product. The contractor stands behind the labor.

WarrantyWho signs itWhat it is about
Manufacturer (material)The shingle makerThe product. The Hawaii brochure says most manufacturer guarantees are “for merchantability – the product is what they say it is.”
Contractor workmanship (labor)The roofing companyThe installation. The same brochure says contractor guarantees “usually apply only to water tightness and not appearance.”

The two do not overlap the way most people assume. A shingle can be made perfectly and still leak at a flashing because of how it was put on. That is a workmanship question, and the manufacturer’s base warranty is not where it gets answered.

Does the shingle manufacturer cover installation mistakes?

Under the base warranty, the sources we read say no. GAF’s homeowner warranty page lists workmanship as “Not covered” for its Shingle & Accessory, Roofing System and System Plus limited warranties. A footnote on GAF’s warranty comparison chart reads: “Some limited workmanship coverage may be available through your contractor.” The same chart says contractors in GAF’s certification programs “are not employees or agents of GAF.”

The wording of the warranty itself points the same way. GAF’s current Shingle & Accessory Limited Warranty (RESWT160L-0426) excludes damage from “improper fastening of your shingles or accessories or application not in strict accordance with GAF’s printed application instructions, if the improper installation was the cause of the damage.” Warranty text changes, so read the document that comes with your roof.

The exception to know about. Some manufacturers back workmanship through an enhanced warranty sold only through credentialed contractors. GAF lists workmanship coverage on its Silver Pledge, Golden Pledge and President’s Club tiers, each tied to a certification level. Owens Corning’s Platinum Protection document lists a Lifetime workmanship period for a single-family detached home with its lifetime shingles, prorated after the first 25 years, and says the contractor is responsible for the first two years of workmanship. It also says the warranty “must be registered by Purchaser’s Owens Corning Roofing Platinum Preferred Contractor with Owens Corning within 60 days of the installation.” CertainTeed says contractors with its ShingleMaster PRO or PREMIER designation can offer SureStart PLUS 4-Star or 5-Star coverage. Those are a different product from the contractor’s own warranty. Our guide to roof shingle warranty tiers explains how the tiers are laid out.

How long is a typical contractor workmanship warranty?

Terms vary, so read yours. CertainTeed’s consumer page says “Contractor workmanship warranties typically are for one to five years.” That is a manufacturer describing contractors, and we found no regulator or trade source that sets a standard length. Whatever number a bid prints is the number being offered, and a longer number only matters if the company is still around to honor it.

Why is a workmanship warranty only as good as the company?

The Hawaii brochure says it plainly: “A guarantee is only as good as the company that issues it.” It continues: “If the company goes out of business, your guarantee becomes ineffective.”

Consider what that means when you compare bids. A long term on paper says nothing about whether the company will exist in year eight. This is our reading, not a sourced rule, but it follows from the quotation: the warranty is a promise from a business, so the business is part of what you are buying. The Indiana Attorney General’s consumer protection office says in its storm-chaser guidance: “Opt for the local, well-established contractor.” Ohio’s Attorney General warned in a June 2014 release that “fly-by-night contractors may descend on a community offering great deals but doing poor work or nothing at all.”

Questions that test the company behind the promise:

  • Who issues it? Ask for the legal name of the company that signs the warranty, and check that it matches the name on the contract and the license.
  • How long has that company operated? Ask for it in writing.
  • Where is the office? Ask for the address and the phone number a claim would go to.
  • Is a manufacturer-backed option available on this roof? If a credentialed contractor can register an enhanced warranty, the manufacturer is a second party on the paper. Ask which one and ask who registers it.

What should a workmanship warranty say in writing?

The Indiana Attorney General’s guidance says: “If a contractor guarantees labor and/or materials, those warranties should be in writing.” Connecticut’s Attorney General says promises like these are “only enforceable if in writing.” A verbal “we stand behind our work” is not a warranty. Use the list below to read the actual document.

Line to look forWhat to look for
LengthThe number of years, and the date it starts (the day work is finished, or the day it is paid).
What is coveredLeaks, flashing, penetrations such as vents and chimneys. Ask whether the repair covers labor and materials.
ExclusionsRead every one. The Hawaii brochure asks whether the warranty is cancelled “if the roof is penetrated in any way, like adding solar energy devices.”
MaintenanceThe brochure asks: “Does it call for maintenance on your part?” Ask what you must do to keep it in force.
TransferabilityThe brochure asks: “Is it transferable to the next owner?” Ask whether there is a fee and a deadline.
Response timeHow quickly the company will respond to a leak report, in writing. If the document is silent, ask.
How to make a claimWho to contact, by what method, and what to send. Keep the phone number and email in your records.
Who issues itThe legal name of the company, as above, and who the manufacturer-backed warranty, if any, is registered to.

The contract and warranty should name the same shingle line, the same scope and the same company. Our contract checklist and bid comparison worksheet have rows for the warranty length and the written terms.

What should I ask each roofing contractor about the workmanship warranty?

Ask every bidder the same questions, in writing, and compare the answers side by side. When every bid prices the same scope, the warranty section is one of the places bids can differ.

  • Is the workmanship warranty in the contract, and can I read the whole document before I sign?
  • How many years does it run, and when does it start?
  • What exactly does it cover: leaks, flashing, penetrations, labor, materials?
  • What does it exclude, and what would cancel it?
  • Is it transferable if I sell the house, and is there a fee?
  • What is your response time when I report a leak, and who do I call?
  • What is the legal name of the company that issues it, and how long has it operated?
  • Can a manufacturer-backed warranty be registered on this roof, who registers it, and when will I get the confirmation?
  • Are there any ways the warranty could be void that are not on the page?

Keep the signed contract, the warranty, the final invoice and photos of the finished roof together. If the company’s name or phone number changes later, you will want proof of whom you hired and when.
