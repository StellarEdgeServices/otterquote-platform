Roof, Gutters, Siding and Windows: One Contractor or Separate Bids?

Otter Quotes · Updated November 2026

← All Guides

One contractor or several is the smaller question. What matters more is whether every trade is priced on its own line and who answers for the places where two trades meet: roof and gutters, roof and siding, windows and siding. Get both answers in writing before you compare any totals.

Should you hire one contractor or collect separate bids?

Both can work, and the choice is yours. One contractor means one contract and one person to call. Separate bids mean a contract for each trade and more comparing. Either way, insist on a price for each part of the job on its own line, so you can compare trade by trade and see what each part costs.

Neither route settles who does the work. A company that sells several trades may use its own crews for some and subcontractors for others. Have every contractor tell you in writing which parts their own crew will do and which they will hand to someone else.

Where do the trades meet on a house?

The trades meet in more places than any bid will list. Three of them are the roof edge and the gutters, the roof and the siding, and the windows and the siding. Water has to be directed away at each one, and manufacturers and technical groups publish instructions for those joints, which is why the joints belong in a bid.

Roof and gutters. GAF's technical bulletin on drip edges describes drip edge as "a metal flashing that directs water away from the edge of the roof," and says it helps keep water from getting behind the soffit edge or siding. It also says: "If there is a gutter edge, make sure the shingles overhang the inside edge of the gutter," so that water goes into the gutter and not down the face of the building. The roofer and the gutter installer are working on the same edge.

Roof and siding. Where a roof ends against a wall, a roofing crew and a siding crew both work the same spot. Settle before the job starts which of them owns the flashing there, and get that into the bid.

Windows and siding. The Building America Solution Center, a U.S. Department of Energy resource, says windows and doors are "an interruption in the wall's house wrap or insulated sheathing drainage plane." It tells builders to "properly integrate all flashing with the drainage plane layer" and to "visually inspect the window and door flashing before siding is installed." Once the siding is on, that flashing is out of sight.

Does the order of the work matter?

Sometimes it does, and the bid should say what order the contractor expects. At the roof edge, ARMA says drip edge generally goes under the underlayment along the eaves and over it on the rakes, and GAF says shingles are installed over the drip edge. At a window, the flashing is inspected before the siding covers it.

You do not have to plan the sequence yourself. Each bid should state which part of the job comes first, what has to be finished before the next trade starts, and who inspects the joint before it is covered. With separate bids, find out which other work each contractor assumes is already done.

The schedule matters too. A written start window for each trade shows conflicts before you sign, so settle what happens if one of them slips.

Who is responsible if a leak shows up where two trades meet?

Settle this before you sign, because the answer depends on the contracts and warranties involved. A leak at a joint may involve two contractors and two manufacturers. Get each contractor to state in writing which parts of the joint their warranty covers and which it does not.

Warranties come with conditions. The National Roofing Contractors Association (NRCA) advises homeowners to read a roofing warranty closely and understand the conditions that would void it.

Put one question to every contractor in the same words: "If water gets in where your work meets another trade's work, whose warranty applies, and what would void it?" The answers show how each one thinks about the joint. Our guide to the contractor workmanship warranty explains what a workmanship warranty covers.

What about older homes and lead-safe rules?

If your home was built before 1978, lead-safe work rules may apply to the job. EPA says its Renovation, Repair and Painting rule covers anyone paid to perform work that disturbs painted surfaces in homes built before 1978, including all firms, even sole proprietorships. Whether a given job disturbs paint is a question for each contractor.

The federal rule's definition of renovation, in 40 CFR 745.83, lists window repair and the removal of building components such as windows among its examples. Siding is not named there. For a siding bid, the useful question is whether the work will disturb painted surfaces and how the crew will contain and clean up dust.

How do you compare bids that cover different trades?

Compare each trade against the other bids for that trade, then look at the total. Three bids are not a comparison if they price three different jobs. A bundled number for roof, gutters and siding cannot be set beside three single-trade bids until it is broken into separate lines.

Decide what you want first, then send the same specs to every contractor so the bids compare apples to apples. Do not treat the lowest total as the answer. NRCA advises a healthy skepticism about the lowest bid and says price is only one criterion for selecting a roofing contractor. A lower total may price fewer parts, a different material or a thinner warranty.

What to ask every contractor

Copy this list into a note and send it to each contractor before you compare totals.

  • Which parts of this job will your own crew do, and which will be subcontracted?
  • Can you price each trade on its own line, so I can compare it to other bids?
  • In what order will the work happen, and what must be finished before the next trade starts?
  • Where the roof meets the gutters, and where the roof or windows meet the siding, who is responsible for the flashing and the joint?
  • Who inspects the flashing before it is covered?
  • If water gets in where your work meets another trade's work, whose warranty applies, and what would void it?
  • Which warranties come with each part, who backs each one, and can I see the documents?
  • My home was built before 1978. How do you follow the EPA's lead-safe work rules?
  • What are your license number and proof of insurance?
  • What does the bid leave out, and how are extras priced and agreed?
  • Who pulls any permits, and is the cost in the price?
  • When can you start, how long will each part take, and what happens if the schedule slips?

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to hire one contractor for the roof, gutters, siding and windows?

Neither is better in every case. One contractor gives you one contract and one point of contact. Separate bids give you a contract for each trade. In both cases, get each part priced on its own line and find out which work the contractor's own crew will do.

Why do the roof and the gutters need to be planned together?

They share the roof edge. GAF's technical bulletin says drip edge directs water away from the edge of the roof, and that where there is a gutter, shingles should overhang its inside edge so water goes into the gutter. Settle who is responsible for that edge when two companies are involved.

What should I check about warranties when several trades work on one house?

Find out which parts of the work each contractor's warranty covers, who backs it, and what would void it. Then settle whose warranty applies if a leak appears where two trades meet. Get the answers and the warranty documents in writing before you sign anything.

Do lead-safe rules apply to siding and window work?

EPA says its rule covers anyone paid to perform work that disturbs painted surfaces in homes built before 1978. The federal definition of renovation names window repair and the removal of building components such as windows; it does not name siding. If your home is older than that, confirm with each contractor whether the job will disturb paint.

Can I compare a bundled bid with separate bids?

Yes, if the bundled bid shows each trade as its own line, with the same scope and the same level of detail. Without separate lines, you cannot see what each part is priced at. Fill in the same comparison table for every contractor.
