My Roof Insurance Check Is Made Out to Me and My Mortgage Company. How Do I Pay the Contractor?

Otter Quotes · Updated October 2026

← All Guides

When a home has a mortgage, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says the insurance company generally writes the settlement check to both you and your mortgage servicer. Your servicer has its own steps for releasing the money. Ask for them in writing, then ask each contractor whether the payment schedule in the bid can follow them.

Why is the insurance check made out to me and my mortgage company?

The CFPB says a home insurance company generally pays a settlement with a check made out to both you and your mortgage servicer or lender, and that most mortgage agreements require this to protect the lender's interest. Your loan documents and your policy decide what applies to you, so read them or ask.

Here is the CFPB's wording:

Your homeowner’s insurance company generally pays your settlement with a check made out to both you and your mortgage servicer or lender. Most mortgage agreements require this to protect the lender’s interest.

Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, “How do home insurance companies pay out claims?”

A state insurance department explains the same arrangement from the loan side. The New Jersey Department of Banking and Insurance says claim checks are likely to be made payable jointly to the customer and the mortgagee, and that the mortgage holder “is listed as the loss payee on the insurance policy, and is included on the check because they have a secured interest in the property.” That page is written for New Jersey customers and carries a 2011 copyright date, so treat it as one explanation, not as a rule for your loan.

What does my mortgage servicer do with the check?

It depends on the servicer, so ask. The CFPB says the servicer typically releases part of the money before work begins so you can hire a contractor, more as the work progresses, and the rest once the job is finished and the home passes inspection. Ask your servicer for its written procedure.

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, a federal regulator of national banks, says banks have different policies and procedures for releasing insurance claim funds. It adds that the loan documents signed at mortgage closing generally give the bank the authority to monitor the work and verify that repairs have been completed before sending funds. The New Jersey page says a mortgage holder may release funds in increments and may order inspections of the property.

Ask your servicer what steps to take with the check. The OCC says that once you receive the check, you should “notify your bank to determine what steps should be taken, including whether you should send the check to the bank.” The CFPB also notes that if you have a mortgage, you are still responsible for making your payments while your insurance claim is paid out.

Copy these questions for your servicer and add your own:

  • Can you send me your written procedure for insurance claim checks?
  • What are the steps for the check itself, such as signing and depositing it?
  • Is the money released all at once or in stages, and what starts each release?
  • Do you order an inspection, who pays for it, and how long does it take to schedule?
  • What paperwork do you need from me and from the contractor before each release?
  • Are released funds sent to me, to the contractor or to both?
  • Who is my contact, and how long does each step take?

Write down the date, the name of the person you spoke with and what they said. Ask for the answers in writing when a phone call leaves anything unclear.

Should I ask my servicer about the money before I collect bids?

The CFPB says to check with your insurance company and your mortgage company or servicer about how and when insurance funds are distributed before you hire contractors. Asking for bids is a separate step from signing, so you can collect bids while you learn the servicer's steps. Read each contract in full before you sign.

Having the servicer's steps in hand first lets you hold each bid's payment terms up against them. How can the contractor's payment schedule follow my servicer's release schedule?

Ask each contractor, before you sign, whether the payment schedule in the bid can follow your servicer's release schedule. A contractor may be able to write the schedule that way. Give the contractor your servicer's written steps, and ask for the agreed schedule to appear in the contract itself.

A bid may split payment into a deposit, progress payments tied to stages of the work, and a final payment at completion. Your servicer may release money in stages too. Matching the two is a conversation between you, the contractor and the servicer, and your signed contract sets out when and how the contractor is paid.

If a contractor's schedule does not match your servicer's, you can ask for a different schedule, or compare a bid from another contractor.

What should I look for in the payment terms of a bid?

Look for four things in writing: the size of any deposit, what each progress payment is tied to, when the final payment is due, and what lien waiver the contractor provides. Ask the questions below, and write the answers next to each bid so the bids compare side by side.

Payment term Question to ask the contractor
DepositIs a deposit part of this bid, how large is it, and what does it pay for?
Progress paymentsWhich stage of the work starts each progress payment, and how is that stage confirmed?
Final paymentWhen is the final payment due, and what needs to be finished first?
Lien waiverWhat lien waiver do you provide, when do I receive it, and what does it cover?
Price changesWhat could change the price after I sign, and how would a change be written down?
ReceiptsCan you give me a receipt for every payment?

The CFPB's page on working with contractors after a disaster tells readers to record “A total price and a schedule for any payments”. It lists, as “Don’ts,” making any upfront payments without a signed contract, paying with cash, and making the final payment until the work is completed to your satisfaction. It lists, as “Do’s,” getting a receipt for all payments and keeping documentation.

What should I ask about lien waivers?

Ask each contractor what lien waiver it provides, when you receive each one, and what each one covers. Ask for the answer in writing. For your state's rules on lien waivers, ask your state consumer protection office or an attorney before you pay.

The CFPB adds that if a contract is for a significant amount of money, you may want to have it reviewed by a lawyer. It also says to never sign a contract without carefully reading it, and that you can always walk away if you feel uncomfortable.

What if the contractor's bid is different from the insurer's estimate?

The bid and the insurer's estimate come from different people. A difference is a question to ask, not a verdict. How any insurance payment relates to the contract price is a question for your insurer and your policy.
