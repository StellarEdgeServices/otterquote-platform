How Long Does It Take to Replace a Roof?

The whole project, from first estimate to final inspection, takes longer, because bids, the contract, a permit, material delivery and scheduling come first. Ask each bidder for dates in writing.

What are the phases of a roof replacement, from first estimate to finished roof?

A roof replacement has eight phases: getting bids, signing a contract, a permit where one is required, ordering and delivering materials, getting on the crew’s schedule, tear-off and installation, inspection, and cleanup. The work on the roof is one or two of them, and the others happen around it.

How long does the work on the roof itself take?

GAF, a shingle manufacturer, says an asphalt shingle roof replacement on an average-sized home “can often be completed in one to two days.” It also says tear-off “is typically accomplished in a few hours,” and that “many factors can influence how long the replacement actually takes.”

That figure covers asphalt shingles. Metal, tile, slate and other systems have their own installation methods, and this page does not give timing for them. If your bid names another product, ask the bidder how many days it expects on the roof and why.

GAF lists these as things that add time on the roof:

  • Rotted decking. Once the old roof is off, the contractor can see whether water has rotted the wood. GAF says damaged or rotting plywood will need to be replaced, which can add “anywhere from a few hours to an extra day.”
  • Weather. GAF says “If rain is in the forecast, this could cause your roofing installation to take longer,” and that crews may complete part of the roof and return the next day. It also says frost on the roof can make it dangerous. Our guide on replacing a roof in winter covers shingle makers’ cold-weather steps.
  • Pitch and complexity. On a steeper roof, GAF says crews “won’t be able to work as fast as they could on a lower-pitched roof,” and roof features that need more cut shingles add time.
  • Access. GAF says limited access can make it harder to position dumpsters, stage materials and dispose of waste, and that preparing the space can add hours at the start.
  • Ventilation. GAF says that if the existing ventilation needs correcting, doing so during installation adds time.

How long does a roofing permit take?

It depends on your building department. Review times vary by city and county, and they can stretch when many applications arrive at once, such as after a storm. No single figure applies everywhere, so this page gives none. Ask the department how long its review takes, and ask each bidder who pulls the permit.

Other questions worth asking: Does my project need a permit at all? How long is the permit good for once it is issued? When must inspections be finished, and who requests them?

The bid should say who handles the permit and when.

What stretches the time between signing and the first day of work?

Three things set the gap: the permit, the arrival of materials, and the contractor’s crew calendar. Each is something a bidder can tell you about before you sign.

After a storm, many roofs need work at the same time, so crews can fill up and permit reviews can slow. Our guide on what to do when roofers are backed up after a storm has questions to ask about start dates.

Do not climb onto the roof yourself to check progress.

What should I ask every roofer about the schedule?

Send the same questions to every bidder and ask for written answers:

  • What is your proposed start date, and how many working days do you expect on the roof?
  • Who requests the permit, and how long does the building department take to issue one?
  • When will materials be ordered and delivered, and what is the plan if they are late?
  • What happens to the dates if rain or cold delays the job?
  • How do you handle rotted decking, and how does it change the schedule and the bid?
  • Who requests each inspection, and when will I know it passed?
  • What does cleanup include, and when is the final walk-through?

How does getting bids on one written scope help me compare schedules?

When every contractor bids on the same written scope, each one states its own schedule in writing against the same job. You can compare start dates and expected days side by side. Our bid comparison worksheet has room for those dates.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to replace a roof?

The full project from first estimate to final inspection is longer, because bids, the contract, any permit, material delivery and scheduling come first. Ask each bidder for a start date and a completion date in writing.

How long does a roofing permit take?

It depends on your building department. Review times vary by city and county, and they can stretch when many applications arrive at once, such as after a storm. Ask the department how long its review takes, and ask each bidder who pulls the permit and when they expect it to be issued.

What can make a roof replacement take longer?

GAF names rain in the forecast, rotted decking found once the old roof is off, a steeper pitch, features that need more cut shingles, tight access for the dumpster and materials, and ventilation fixes. Permits and a crowded contractor schedule can add time before work starts. Ask each bidder what in your roof could stretch the job.

Why do two roof bids give different start dates?

Start dates reflect the contractor’s own schedule, how soon materials can be ordered and delivered, and when the permit is expected. Two bidders on the same scope can differ on all three. Ask each one to write down the start date, how long the work should take and what happens to the dates if weather delays the job.

What should a roof bid say about the schedule?

A bid can state the expected start date, how many days the work should take, when materials will be ordered, who requests the permit and inspections, and what happens if weather or rotted decking changes the plan. If a bid is silent on any of these, ask before you compare totals. Written dates let you compare bids on the same footing.
