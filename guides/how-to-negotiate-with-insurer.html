Roof Insurance Claim Settlement: Key Terms and Questions to Ask

The terms that appear on a roof insurance estimate, who is involved at each step, and questions you can ask your insurer.

Dustin Stohler
Co-founder & CEO, Otter Quotes  ·  Updated May 2026

Insurance adjusters and roofing contractors look at the same storm damage and can produce estimates that differ. Differences can come from different measurement methods and different line-item pricing, and from the fact that an adjuster prepares the insurer's estimate while a contractor prepares a bid for the work. This page explains the terms you will see on those documents and lists questions you can ask your insurer.

This page covers the terms on a roof insurance estimate, who is involved at each step, and how to compare contractor bids on the same scope of work.

1

Know the Terms in Your Policy

Insurance documents use terms that are easy to mix up. Two of them are Actual Cash Value (ACV) and Replacement Cost Value (RCV). Your policy and your insurer are the source for which one applies to your roof.

Coverage Type How It Works Where to Look
ACV A way of valuing the roof that accounts for depreciation based on its age and condition Your policy and the insurer's written estimate show which method is used
RCV A way of valuing the cost to replace the damaged property with new materials of like kind and quality Your policy and the insurer's written estimate show which method is used

Recoverable depreciation is a term for a depreciation amount that a policy or an estimate may list separately from the replacement cost. Whether and when any amount is payable is stated in your policy and decided by your insurer. Ask your insurer or agent how your policy treats it.

Other terms you may see in your policy, and questions to ask your insurer or agent about them:

  • Coverage exclusions: Policies define storm damage and wear and tear in their own words. Ask your insurer or agent where your policy describes them.
  • Ordinance or law coverage: A policy term that relates to building-code requirements when a roof is replaced. Ask your insurer or agent whether your policy includes it and what it says.
  • Matching provisions: A policy term that relates to matching materials on damaged and undamaged sections. Ask your insurer or agent whether your policy includes one and what it says.
Your declarations page is a good place to start. It is a summary of your coverage limits and coverage types. If you can't find it, ask your insurer for a copy.
2

Keep Your Own Record of the Damage

A written record of the damage is useful to you: it shows each contractor the same condition, and it is yours to keep. Photograph and video the roof and property with timestamps turned on.

Start from the ground. If climbing is unsafe, do not climb; you can ask a contractor to look at the roof surface while preparing a bid. Photograph and video every area with visible damage:

  • Shingles: Missing tabs, bruised or dented shingles (hail impacts look like small craters with exposed fiberglass mat), cracked or curling edges
  • Flashings and penetrations: Chimney flashing, pipe boots, skylights, and valley flashing are areas to look at closely
  • Gutters and downspouts: Look for dents or dings from hail impact
  • Soffit and fascia: Note any displaced, dented, or cracked sections
  • Interior water intrusion: Photograph any water stains, bubbling paint, or wet insulation visible from inside, particularly in the attic

Create a folder with all photos organized by area. Include photos with timestamps enabled. You can share the folder with contractors so each one bids on the same condition.

Photo and Record Checklist

  • Photographs of damaged shingles from multiple angles
  • Photos of flashing, pipe boots, and all roof penetrations
  • Photos of gutters and downspouts showing hail impact
  • Photos of any soffit, fascia, or siding damage
  • Interior attic photos showing any moisture or daylight intrusion
  • Date and location of the storm documented (weather service records or local news reports)
  • Copy of your declarations page and policy number in one place

Know Who Is Involved at Each Step

After a storm, different people look at the same roof for different reasons. An insurance adjuster represents the insurer and prepares the insurer's estimate. A roofing contractor prepares a bid for the work they would do. Otter Quotes does neither: we create a written scope of work for your project and send it to multiple contractors.

Questions you can ask your insurer about its process:

  • Who is my contact? Ask who handles your claim and how to reach them.
  • Can I have a copy of the written estimate? Ask how and when you will receive it.
  • Where in my policy are the terms the estimate refers to? Ask your insurer or agent to point you to them.
  • What documents do you need from me? Ask what the insurer accepts and how to send it.

A contractor you invite can walk the roof with you and explain what they would include in their own bid. Questions about the insurer's estimate belong with your insurer.

4

Read an Estimate and a Contractor Bid Side by Side

An insurer's written estimate and a contractor's bid are different documents with different purposes. Your insurer can answer questions about its estimate; a contractor can answer questions about a bid. Each is prepared with its own pricing and measurements, so line items can differ.

Ask each contractor to explain their bid line by line, and to tell you what it includes and excludes, such as:

  • Pricing: how each material and labor line is priced in their bid
  • Items that may be missing from a bid: pipe boots, ice and water shield, drip edge, valley flashing. Ask whether each is included.
  • Measurements: how the contractor measured the roof, and whether the square footage matches what other bids use
  • Code-related work: ask whether upgraded underlayment or specific fastening patterns that local code requires are included in the bid

Bids from different contractors can differ because their scope, materials or measurements differ. Ask each contractor to explain any difference you see, and ask which items belong in the scope of work.

When comparing bids and estimates, look at:

  • Squares of roofing material — one square = 100 sq ft; ask each contractor how they measured your roof
  • Material line items — synthetic underlayment vs. felt, drip edge, ridge cap shingles, starter strips, pipe flashing boots
  • Labor rates — tear-off and disposal, re-decking if needed, flashing replacement labor
  • Overhead and profit (O&P) — a line that some estimates and bids list separately; ask what it covers
5

What a Supplement Is

A supplement is a term used in claims for a request to revise an existing estimate, for example to add line items or change pricing. Whether to ask for one, and what it requires, is between you and your insurer. Otter Quotes does not prepare or submit supplements.

Questions you can ask your insurer about its process:

  • Does the insurer have a process for changes to a written estimate? Ask how it works and who to contact.
  • What does it need in writing? Ask what format and documents it accepts.
  • Is there a time limit? Ask your insurer or agent whether your policy sets one.

Ask your insurer to confirm anything it tells you in writing, and keep your own records.

Terms vary. Insurers describe changes to a written estimate in their own words. Use the words in your own policy and estimate when you ask questions.

Questions about your claim, your payments, or your legal rights belong with your insurer or, for legal questions, an attorney licensed in your state.

6

What a Re-Inspection Is

A re-inspection is a term for a second inspection of a property after a first claim inspection. Whether one is available, who performs it and how it is requested are questions for your insurer.

Questions you can ask your insurer:

  • Does the insurer offer a second inspection, and how is one requested?
  • Who attends, and may I or a contractor I choose be present?
  • How will the result be communicated to me in writing?

Your own records of the roof's condition, such as your photos and any contractor bids, are yours to keep and share as you choose.

Before any repair starts, ask your insurer what it needs and ask your contractor about temporary protection for the roof. Keep receipts for any temporary work.
7

What an Appraisal Clause Is

Some policies include language often called an appraisal clause, which describes a way to resolve a disagreement about the amount of a loss. Whether yours does, and what it says, is in your policy.

Questions you can ask your insurer or agent:

  • Does my policy have an appraisal clause? Ask where it is and what it says.
  • What does the policy say about how it works and what it costs? Ask your insurer or agent to point you to the language.

An appraisal clause is a policy process. Otter Quotes does not take part in it or advise on it.

For legal questions about your rights under your policy, consult an attorney licensed in your state.

8

What a Public Adjuster Is

Some homeowners hire a professional to work on their claim. This section explains the roles so you know who is who.

A public adjuster (PA) is a licensed professional hired by and working for the homeowner, rather than for the insurer. An insurance adjuster works for the insurer. Public adjusters are licensed at the state level, and fee arrangements vary; ask for the terms in writing before signing anything.

Questions to ask before hiring anyone to work on your claim:

  • What license do you hold, and in which state?
  • How are you paid? Please put it in writing.
  • Can I speak with references from recent clients?
  • What exactly will you do for my claim?

A property damage attorney is a lawyer licensed to give legal advice. Legal questions about your policy or your insurer's conduct belong with an attorney licensed in your state. Otter Quotes cannot advise on them.

If someone offers to handle your insurance claim for you, ask what license they hold. Licensing rules for that work are set by each state, and your state's Department of Insurance can tell you what applies. Ask any contractor to explain the scope and quality of their work in writing.

If you are considering hiring a public adjuster, verify their license with your state's Department of Insurance, ask for references from recent clients, and check whether any complaints have been filed against them. Ask them to explain exactly what they will do for your specific claim before you sign anything.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between ACV and RCV on a roof insurance claim?

ACV (Actual Cash Value) is a way of valuing a roof that accounts for its age and wear. RCV (Replacement Cost Value) is a way of valuing the cost to replace damaged material with new material of like kind and quality. Some policies and estimates also use the term recoverable depreciation for a depreciation amount that is listed separately. Your policy states which terms apply, and your insurer or agent can explain them.

A contractor's bid is the contractor's own document; questions about your claim or your coverage belong with your insurer.
