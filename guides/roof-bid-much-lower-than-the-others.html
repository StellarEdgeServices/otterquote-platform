Why Is One Roof Bid Much Lower Than the Others?

A roof bid far below the others usually prices a different job: fewer lines, a lower material tier, a thinner warranty or missing credentials. Sometimes it reflects a real difference in how the contractor works. The total cannot tell you which, so set the bid beside the others line by line, ask the bidder to answer in writing, then decide.

Why can one roof bid be so much lower than the others?

A much lower roof bid usually prices a different job. It may leave out lines the other bids include, such as tear-off, decking, underlayment, flashing or ventilation. It may quote a lower material tier, a thinner warranty, or a crew without the same credentials. Less often, it reflects a real difference in how the contractor works.

Our post on why roofers quote different prices covers the ordinary reasons honest bids differ. This page covers one situation: a single bid sits well below the rest. The first step is to put all the bids on the roof bid comparison worksheet, the low one included, and write “not stated” wherever a bid is silent.

Which missing lines can explain a low roof bid?

Any line a bid leaves out is a cost it does not carry. The table pairs common lines with what manufacturer and government sources say, and what to look for in the low bid.

Line What a source says What to look for in the low bid
Tear-off of old roofing The GAF Pro Field Guide says “In some situations, roofing over an old roof may be acceptable,” and also that tear-off is required on every installation that carries its Golden Pledge or Silver Pledge warranty. Does it remove the old roofing, or install over it? How many layers does it remove?
Decking GAF’s installation instructions say roof decks “must be dry prior to installation of shingles,” and its field guide says to “Repair and replace any damaged or rotted sections” before installing roof deck protection. Is rotted decking priced per sheet, covered by an allowance, or excluded?
Underlayment GAF’s installation instructions say underlayment “is required by many code bodies and is required to maintain the shingles’ UL Class A fire rating.” Is an underlayment listed, and is it named by type?
Leak barrier (ice and water) The instructions say “At eaves and where ice dams can be expected,” use a leak barrier. Which areas does it cover: eaves, valleys, around penetrations?
Drip edge and starter The field guide says GAF “strongly recommends that drip edge be installed at the rakes and eaves on all GAF shingle roofs,” and the instructions call for starter strip shingles “along the eaves and rake.” Are both listed, and is the starter from the shingle maker’s own line?
Flashing Owens Corning’s installation instructions say step flashing “should be a corrosion resistant metal” where the roof meets sidewalls or chimneys, and GAF’s instructions give separate steps for wall and chimney flashing. Does it list flashing at walls and chimneys, and does it say new flashing or reuse of what is there?
Ventilation GAF’s instructions say “Proper underdeck ventilation is essential to reduce moisture build up and prevent mold.” What ventilation does the bid install or add?
Permit and disposal The Ohio Attorney General says a contract should include “A requirement for the contractor to obtain all necessary permits and inspections.” The FTC lists a request that you get required permits as a sign of a scam. Who pulls the permit, who is named on it, and who hauls away the old roofing?

Requirements differ by manufacturer. Ask the low bidder which manufacturer’s instructions the bid follows.

Can a lower material tier or warranty explain a low bid?

Yes. Two bids can both say “architectural shingles” and name different product lines, and the warranty can change with the product and the installer. A cheaper line, a plain warranty or a thinner workmanship promise can each lower a total without any line being missing.

GAF’s warranty comparison chart shows how this works for one manufacturer. Its base and system coverage can be installed by “Anyone,” while its enhanced coverage is installed only by contractors in GAF’s own contractor program. A footnote on the chart says “Some limited workmanship coverage may be available through your contractor.” So the manufacturer’s warranty and the contractor’s workmanship warranty are two different promises. Ask which of each the low bid includes, and get the workmanship terms in writing.

Can missing licensing or coverage explain a low bid?

It can. Licensing, liability coverage and workers’ compensation coverage are costs of running a roofing business, so a bid from a company without them can look lower for that reason alone. Ask each bidder for proof, and check it yourself with the office or company that issued it.

The National Roofing Contractors Association (NRCA) says to “Ask for copies of the contractor’s liability insurance coverage and workers’ compensation certificates.” The FTC says to work with contractors who are licensed and insured and to check the license with your state or county. Licensing rules can differ by state, county and city, so ask your local building office what applies.

How do I find out which explanation is true?

Compare the low bid to the others line by line, put the same written questions to the low bidder, and judge the answers. The FTC advises getting several estimates and asking about large price differences. The explanation you want is specific, in writing, and matches the lines on the other bids.

  1. Fill in the worksheet for every bid. Mark every blank “not stated.”
  2. Send the low bidder the checklist below, in writing. Send the same list to the other bidders so every answer lands on the same lines.
  3. Ask for proof of license and coverage, and check it with the issuer.
  4. If the low bid left lines out, ask the bidder to re-quote with them included. If the other bids carry extras you did not ask for, ask them to price those separately.
  5. Compare totals only after the lines match.

What should I ask the low bidder in writing?

Ask for written answers, and for the answers to go into the contract. Copy this list:

  • Does the price include tear-off of all existing roofing, and disposal of it?
  • How is rotted decking handled: per sheet, an allowance, or excluded?
  • Which underlayment, which leak barrier and where, and which drip edge, starter and ridge cap products?
  • Do you replace or reuse flashing, and what ventilation do you install?
  • Which shingle manufacturer and product line, and what manufacturer warranty does the bid assume?
  • What workmanship warranty do you give, for how long, and who stands behind it?
  • Who pulls the permit, who is named on it, and who pays for it?
  • Does your own crew do the work, or a subcontractor?
  • What are your license number and proof of liability and workers’ compensation coverage?
  • What is the payment schedule, and what is each payment tied to?
  • How are changes priced, and do you need my written approval first?

What are the red flags in a low roof bid?

The strongest red flags are about money and paperwork, not the price itself: pressure to pay up front or in cash, a large deposit, a request that you pull the permit, or a refusal to put the scope in writing. Any of them is a reason to stop and ask more questions before you sign.

  • Pay everything up front, or cash only. The FTC lists these among the signs of a scam and says not to pay the full amount for the project up front.
  • A large deposit. The Ohio Attorney General says the contract should not require a large down payment or payment in full until the project is complete and inspected.
  • You are asked to get the permit. The FTC lists this as a scam sign, and the Ohio Attorney General says the contract should require the contractor to obtain the permits and inspections.
  • Pressure to decide now. The FTC lists pressure for an immediate decision as a scam sign. A price good only today leaves no time to compare.
  • No full written proposal. The NRCA says to “Insist on a written proposal and look for complete descriptions of the work and specifications.”
  • No proof of license or coverage when you ask. Ask for it, then check it with the office or company that issued it.
  • A low price that may not hold. The Indiana Attorney General warns that a contractor with a low price “may be inexperienced and unable to finish the work for the bid amount.”

Our post on red flags in a roofing estimate covers the wider list. None of these proves a contractor is bad. They tell you where to ask.

When is a much lower roof bid fine?

A lower bid can be fine when, after you match the lines, it covers the same work, the same products, a written workmanship warranty and proof of license and coverage, and the contractor explains the gap without pressure. The NRCA says price is only one criterion, and the FTC says not to automatically choose the lowest bidder.

Neither source says the lowest bid is wrong; the NRCA’s healthy skepticism means checking, not rejecting. A contractor can also have a different cost structure from the others, for example by spending less on sales and marketing, and a bid with matching lines can reflect that. If the lines match and the answers hold up, the lower total is the one worth considering.

How does getting bids on one written scope help me compare?

When every contractor prices the same written scope and the same measurements, the lines match from the start, so a low total has fewer places to hide a missing line. You can see where one bid differs and ask about it, instead of guessing.

Frequently asked questions

Is a roof bid that is much lower than the others a bad sign?

Not by itself, but it is a reason to ask questions. A much lower bid often prices a different job, with fewer lines, a lower material tier, a thinner warranty or missing credentials. The NRCA advises a healthy skepticism about the lowest bid. Line it up against the others and ask the bidder to explain each gap in writing.

How much lower than the other bids is too low?

We found no primary source that sets a percentage, so this page does not give one. The FTC says not to automatically choose the lowest bidder and to ask about large price differences. The size of the gap matters less than whether the bidder can explain it, line by line, in writing.

What should I ask a roofer whose bid is far below the others?

Ask what the price includes: tear-off, decking, underlayment, leak barrier, drip edge, flashing, ventilation, permit and disposal. Ask which shingle line and warranty it assumes, who stands behind the workmanship warranty, and for proof of license and insurance. Ask for every answer in writing, and for the answers to go into the contract.

Can a low roof bid grow after work starts?

It can. The Indiana Attorney General warns that a contractor with a low price may be unable to finish the work for the bid amount. Ask how rotted decking is priced, how change orders are authorized, and whether any change needs your written sign-off before work begins. Get those terms in the contract.

Is a large deposit a warning sign on a low bid?

It can be. The FTC lists asking you to pay for everything up front, or accepting only cash, as signs of a scam, and the Ohio Attorney General says a contract should not require a large down payment or payment in full until the project is complete and inspected. Ask for a payment schedule tied to finished work.
