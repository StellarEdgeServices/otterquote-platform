How to Read a Roofing Contractor Estimate

What each line item on a roofing estimate means, what a scope of work is, and what to check on a written bid before you compare it with another.

Otter Quotes
Updated October 2026

A roofing estimate is a written price for a defined piece of work. Contractors write them in shorthand, such as squares, starter, drip edge and ice and water shield. This guide defines each of those terms, explains the difference between unit pricing and a lump sum, describes what a scope of work is, and lists what to check on a written bid.

Warranty terms have their own page: Roofing Warranty Tiers Explained.

What Are the Line Items on a Roofing Estimate?

The table defines each common line item and, where a building code or a manufacturer addresses it, quotes what that source says. Building codes are adopted by states and local governments, which can change them (one state's rule begins: “Chapter 9 of the IRC® 2018 is adopted with the following modifications”). The requirements for a given roof are therefore the ones your local building department publishes. The code quotes below come from several editions of the International Residential Code (2009 to 2018) as reproduced or summarized by state and local agencies. Editions differ, and your jurisdiction may have adopted another one. They are shown as examples of the model text.

Line Item What It Means What a Code or Manufacturer Source Says
Square A unit of roof area equal to 100 square feet. A roof with 2,400 square feet of roof area is 24 squares. GAF's glossary: “A measurement of roof area equal to 100 square feet or enough roofing material to cover 100 square feet of a roof deck.” (GAF)
Tear-off and disposal Removing the existing roof covering and hauling it away. Model code text (2015 edition, as printed in Seattle’s residential code), R908.3: “Roof replacement shall include the removal of existing layers of roof coverings down to the roof deck.” The next subsection, R908.3.1, permits a new roof covering over an existing one under listed conditions. An exception lets an existing ice barrier membrane that is adhered to the deck stay in place under a new layer. (Seattle Residential Code, Chapter 9)
Shingles The roof covering, named by manufacturer and product line, such as GAF Timberline HDZ. A 3-tab shingle is a single layer cut into three tabs. An architectural (dimensional) shingle is built from multiple laminated layers. GAF: 3-tab shingles are “single-layer shingles” with a “uniform, flat appearance”; “Architectural shingles, like GAF Timberline HDZ® and Timberline® AS II, are made of multiple layers of asphalt-coated fiberglass that are laminated together.” (GAF)
Underlayment A rolled layer installed under the shingles. It is made of asphalt-saturated felt or of synthetic materials. GAF's glossary: “Asphaltic saturated organic or synthetic-based rolled materials designed to be installed under the main roofing materials to help shed water and resist weather infiltration.” (GAF)
Ice and water shield (the code calls it an ice barrier) A self-adhering, “peel-and-stick” leak barrier applied before flashing at eaves and rake edges, valley joints, and around skylights and dormers. GAF describes where it goes (GAF). The model code, R905.1.2: “In areas where there has been a history of ice forming along the eaves causing a backup of water as designated in Table R301.2(1), an ice barrier shall be installed for asphalt shingles,” extending “to a point not less than 24 inches (610 mm) inside the exterior wall line of the building.” (Indiana Department of Homeland Security)
Drip edge Rigid material installed at the edge of the roof, along the eaves and rakes, that sets the shingles out over the eave and gutter. Model code R905.2.8.5: “A drip edge shall be provided at eaves and rake edges of shingle roofs.” (Indiana Department of Homeland Security)
Starter strip The first row of shingles, installed at the eave. GAF's glossary: “The first row of roofing that is installed at the edge of the eave.” (GAF)
Ridge cap Material made to cover the peak (the ridge) and the hips of the roof. GAF's glossary: “Material specifically made to cover the peak ridge or hips of the roof.” (GAF)
Flashing (step, valley, chimney, pipe boots) Material installed around a roof penetration or along an edge to help keep moisture out. A valley is where two downward-sloping sections of roof join. GAF's glossary defines flashing and valley (GAF). Model code R905.2.8.1: “Base and cap flashing shall be installed in accordance with manufacturer's instructions.” (Seattle Residential Code, Chapter 9)
Decking (sheathing) The structural material the roofing is applied over. Plywood and oriented strand board (OSB) are both wood structural panels used for roof sheathing. GAF's glossary: “The structural material over which roofing is applied. Usually plywood, boards, or planks.” Model code R905.2.1: “Asphalt shingles shall be fastened to solidly sheathed decks.” APA: plywood and OSB are “engineered wood structural panels commonly used in floor, wall, and roof systems.” (GAF, Seattle, APA)
Ventilation Openings that let air, heat and water vapor out of the attic, such as ridge vents, soffit vents and box vents. GAF’s glossary, under attic vent: “An opening that allows air, heat, and water vapor to escape from the attic.” Macomb Township Building Department’s re-roof requirements sheet (revised 4/1/2026), which summarizes Michigan’s residential code and cites Sec. R806.2: “The minimum net free ventilation area shall be 1/150 of the area of the vented space. Exception: The minimum net free ventilation area shall be 1/300 of the vented space provided when not less than 40 percent and not more than 50 percent of the required ventilating area is provided by ventilators located in the upper portion of the attic or rafter space.” (GAF, Macomb Township Building Department)
Permit The permission a local building official gives for work the building code regulates. Model code R105.1 (2009 edition): “Any owner or authorized agent who intends to construct, enlarge, alter, repair, move, demolish or change the occupancy of a building or structure … shall first make application to the building official and obtain the required permit.” (2009 IRC, Section R105)
A bid can group related items on one line. When it does, the line should still say what it includes. A single line reading “complete roof replacement” with one price does not say.

Unit Pricing or Lump Sum?

A unit-priced estimate states a price per unit: per square of shingles, per sheet of decking, per linear foot of drip edge. A lump-sum estimate states one price for the whole scope. The Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry lists “the total contract price or how the price will be calculated” among the things a written contract should include, which describes either format (Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry).

A change order is a written change to the contract. The same agency lists “a provision requiring that every change to the contract be reduced to a written change order that you are required to sign” among those items. Look for where a bid says how extra work, such as damaged decking found after the old roof is removed, would be priced and recorded.

What Is a Scope of Work?

A scope of work is the written description of the work a contractor agrees to do. The Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry lists “a detailed summary of the work to be done” and “a description of materials” among the parts of a written contract. Otter Quotes creates a scope of work and submits it to multiple contractors, who then bid.

A written scope can answer these questions:

  • What is replaced and what is reused? A bid can name flashing, pipe boots, valley flashing and step flashing at chimneys as replaced or as reused.
  • Which materials, by manufacturer and product line? “Architectural shingles” names a category. A product line, such as GAF Timberline HDZ, names a product.
  • Which underlayment, and what ventilation plan? The line items above define both.
  • What are the exclusions? An exclusion is an item the bid states it does not include, such as gutter removal or interior repairs.
  • What are the contingencies? A contingency is a condition that can change the price, such as a stated price per sheet if damaged decking is found.
  • How many layers come off? One local building department's re-roof sheet states: “New roof coverings shall not be installed without first removing existing roof coverings where 1) there are 2 or more applications of any type of roof coverings”. (Macomb Township Building Department)

What Do the Material Names on a Bid Mean?

Shingles

GAF describes 3-tab as “the most economical roofing choice” (GAF). The shingles row of the table above quotes GAF on architectural shingles and names Timberline HDZ and Timberline AS II as examples, so a bid that names the manufacturer and the product line says more than one that says “architectural.”

Underlayment

GAF states that underlayment is “usually made of either asphalt-saturated felt or synthetic materials” and acts as “a secondary moisture barrier between the roof deck and the overlying shingles” (GAF). A bid should say which type it uses.

How Do Warranties Show Up on a Bid?

A bid may name a manufacturer warranty, a contractor workmanship warranty, or both. They are two different promises with different sources, and the terms are defined, with GAF's and CertainTeed's own published terms side by side, on the warranty tiers page. On Otter Quotes, the bid comparison lists the workmanship warranty and the manufacturer's warranty as separate rows.

What Should You Check on a Written Bid?

Each item below is something a bid can state in writing. A bid that leaves one out is a bid to ask about.

  • A detailed summary of the work. A single line with one price does not give one. (Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry, above.)
  • The materials, by manufacturer and product line. Including the underlayment type.
  • How damaged decking and other extra work are priced and recorded. See unit pricing and change orders above.
  • Who applies for the permit. The permit row above quotes the 2009 edition of the model code, R105.1, on who makes the application.
  • The payment schedule. The Minnesota agency lists a “payment schedule making clear when payments are due” among the parts of a written contract.
  • The timeline. The same agency lists “specific timelines.” Otter Quotes bid forms ask contractors for an estimated start date and completion time, and the bids page shows both.
  • The warranties, in writing. Which manufacturer warranty, by name, and what workmanship warranty the contractor gives. GAF, for example, states which of its warranties a contractor can offer depending on the contractor's GAF designation (GAF).

Red Flags on a Written Bid

The Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry’s page “Tips for hiring a contractor”, written for Minnesota homeowners, lists contractors to avoid. The list includes those that “ask you to sign an estimate or authorization before you have decided to hire them. You may unknowingly be signing a contract”, “require full or substantial payment before work begins” and “refuse to provide a written estimate or contract” (Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry). The agency’s page has the full list.

How Do You Compare Two Estimates?

  1. Compare scope first. Confirm both bids include the same line items from the table above before you compare totals.
  2. Compare product lines. Two bids that both say “architectural shingles” may name different products.
  3. Compare warranties by document. A bid that says “lifetime warranty” and a bid that says “25-year warranty” may describe different documents from different sources. See the warranty tiers page.
  4. Compare how decking is handled. One bid may state a price per sheet while another states a flat price, so compare how each one records extra work.
  5. Compare square counts. If two bids state different numbers of squares, they are not priced on the same roof area. Ask each contractor where the number came from. On Otter Quotes, homeowners pay as little as $15 up front for professional measurements that are used by every contractor, and the money is refunded if they complete a job on the platform. See Aerial Roof Measurement Reports.
  6. Compare payment terms and timelines. The same total can come with different payment schedules.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many squares of roofing do I need?

A square is 100 square feet of roof area, so divide the roof area in square feet by 100. A roof with 2,400 square feet of roof area is 24 squares. A bid should state the number of squares it assumes.

What is the difference between felt and synthetic underlayment?

Both are rolled layers installed under the shingles. GAF’s description of the two types is quoted in the underlayment section above. A bid should say which one it specifies.

What is a change order?

A change order is a written change to the contract. The Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry’s description of it is quoted in the unit pricing section above.

What does the building code say about attic ventilation?

The ventilation row of the line-item table above quotes a Michigan township’s re-roof sheet on Sec. R806.2, including an exception. Your local building department publishes the version that applies where you live.

How do I check a contractor's manufacturer designation?

Each manufacturer names and defines its own contractor designations. GAF, for example, describes Certified, Certified Plus and Master Elite contractors and its contractor page states: “Enter your contractor’s name or business to verify that they are certified by GAF.” The warranty tiers page lists what each designation can offer.
