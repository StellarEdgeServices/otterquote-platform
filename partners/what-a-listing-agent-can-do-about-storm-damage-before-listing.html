When a seller's roof has storm damage before the house is listed, a listing agent can help the seller get a written scope of work and written bids from more than one contractor on that same scope. Otter Quotes creates the scope of work and sends it to multiple contractors, so the seller can compare bids online and decide.

What are the seller's options when the roof has storm damage before listing?

A seller generally has three paths: repair or replace the roof before listing, list the house as it is, or agree to a price adjustment or credit with the buyer. The seller and the buyer choose among them with their own professionals. Each path is easier to discuss when the seller has a written scope and bids to point to.

OptionWhat it looks likeWhat the seller may want in hand
Repair or replace before listingThe seller hires a contractor and the work is done before showings or before a contractA written scope, written bids on that scope, and the signed contract and any warranty paperwork
List the house as it isThe seller lists with the damage in place and the buyer's side decides what to do about itWritten bids on one scope, so the number being discussed is not a guess
Price adjustment or creditThe parties negotiate an adjustment or credit in place of a repair done by the sellerWritten bids on one scope, as a starting point for the parties and their own professionals

Otter Quotes does not recommend one path over another. The choice depends on the seller's plans, the contract and the advice of the people the seller works with.

What about disclosure of the damage?

Seller disclosure duties differ from state to state and from one transaction to the next. This guide does not say what your state requires, and Otter Quotes does not give legal advice. Ask your broker or your attorney, and read your state's seller disclosure form before you advise a seller on what to say about the roof.

Some states publish an official seller disclosure form with a question about the roof. As one example only, the Ohio Department of Commerce Residential Property Disclosure Form asks:

"Do you know of any previous or current leaks or other material problems with the roof or rain gutters?"

That is Ohio's form. It is not a statement about the form, the questions or the duties in any other state, and it is not advice about a particular sale.

Why get a written scope and bids before the house is listed?

Contractors price a repair or replacement from a scope of work, a written list of the work to be done. One bid gives a seller nothing to compare it to, and bids written for different work do not compare. A written scope sent to several contractors puts the same question to each of them and can get comparable numbers back.

Time matters in a listing. A seller waiting to learn what the roof will cost may also be waiting to set a price or a list date. Getting the scope out early can shorten that wait. It cannot promise how many bids arrive or when, and the contractor sets the repair schedule. Our side-by-side worksheet for roofing bids is a table the seller can fill in, one column per bid.

What can a listing agent hand the seller?

A listing agent can hand the seller a short folder, so the roof does not become a loose end during the listing. Nothing in it replaces advice from your broker or attorney.

  • The written scope of work for the roof, so every bid refers to the same work.
  • Written bids on that scope, side by side, with each contractor's license information and reviews.
  • Material and warranty details from each bid, by manufacturer and product line, and what each warranty covers.
  • The signed contract, the invoice and any permit paperwork, if the seller repairs, kept together and ready to share with the buyer's side.
  • Your state's seller disclosure form, with a note to talk to your broker or attorney about the roof section.

If the seller has an insurance claim open, questions about the claim, the policy or any estimate the insurer wrote are for the seller's insurer. The seller can ask the insurer to explain its own document.

How can bids on one scope help a buyer trust the number?

A buyer's side that sees one repair price from one contractor has to decide whether to take it on faith. Several written bids on the same scope let the buyer's agent see how the prices compare. That does not make any price correct. It gives both sides one document to discuss instead of a number with no source.

The same holds for a credit or price adjustment: each side can review the bids with their own professionals.

What should a listing agent ask?

Copy these into your notes. The first group is for your broker or attorney. The second is for every contractor, so the answers are easier to compare.

For your broker or attorney

  • What does my state's seller disclosure form ask about the roof, and when does the seller complete it?
  • Does my listing agreement or my brokerage's policy say anything about repairs the seller arranges?
  • If the seller repairs, which records should be kept and shared?

For each contractor

  • Does this bid cover the same written scope as the other bids?
  • Which materials are included, by manufacturer and product line?
  • What does the warranty cover, for how long, and who stands behind it? Can it be passed to the next owner?
  • What license information can you show me, and where can I look it up?
  • Will your own crew do the work, or a subcontractor?
  • What is not included in this bid?
  • What are the payment terms, and what is the proposed start date?
