Public Adjuster vs. Handling Your Roof Claim Yourself

A public adjuster works for you instead of the insurer — for a percentage of the payout. Here's a neutral look at the trade-offs.

By Dustin Stohler · June 2, 2026

← All Articles

When a roof claim gets complicated, some homeowners hire a public adjuster — a licensed professional who represents you, not the insurance company. Others handle the claim themselves. There's no single right answer; it depends on the size and complexity of the loss, your comfort with paperwork, and what your state allows. This guide explains what a public adjuster actually does and lays out the trade-offs so you can have an informed conversation with the right professional.

What a Public Adjuster Actually Does

A public adjuster is a licensed insurance professional who works on behalf of the policyholder — the homeowner — rather than the carrier. Their role typically includes reviewing your policy, documenting the damage, preparing and submitting the claim, and negotiating the settlement with the insurer's adjuster.

They're distinct from two other adjusters you may encounter: the company (staff) adjuster who works for your insurer, and the independent adjuster the insurer hires to inspect on its behalf. Only the public adjuster represents your interests in the claim.

What They Cost

Public adjusters usually work on contingency — a percentage of the amount you recover. The percentage varies, and several states cap it, especially for declared-disaster claims. Because the fee comes out of your settlement, the practical question is whether their involvement increases the payout by more than they charge.

Worth checking first: many states regulate public adjuster fees and contracts, and some impose limits after a catastrophe. Confirm the rules where you live and read any contract carefully before signing.

When Homeowners Often Handle It Themselves

For a straightforward roof claim, many homeowners manage the process on their own. The job is largely organization and documentation:

  • Document the damage with dated photos and notes as soon as it's safe.
  • Report the claim to your carrier and keep a written log of every call and email.
  • Be present for the adjuster's inspection and share your documentation.
  • Get a detailed contractor estimate so you understand the scope and cost of repairs.
  • Review the insurer's estimate against the damage and ask questions about anything missing.

When the damage is clear and the insurer's offer is reasonable, this is often enough — with no fee taken out of the settlement.

When People Consider Bringing One In

The case for a public adjuster tends to grow with the size and difficulty of the claim. Homeowners more often consider one when:

  • The loss is large or complex — significant structural damage, not just a few shingles.
  • The claim has been denied or underpaid and they believe the damage was missed.
  • The policy language is complicated, or there's a dispute over what's covered.
  • They simply don't have the time or bandwidth to manage a drawn-out claim.

Even then, it's a personal cost-benefit decision: the potential increase in the settlement against the contingency fee.

A Boundary Worth Being Clear About

One reason this topic causes confusion is that some contractors offer to "handle your claim" or "negotiate with your adjuster." In most states, negotiating a claim on a homeowner's behalf is a licensed public-adjusting activity — not something a roofer is permitted to do simply because they're doing the repair. Be cautious with anyone who blurs that line, and especially with anyone who offers to "waive your deductible," which can be insurance fraud.

Questions to Ask Before You Decide

Whether you're leaning toward hiring help or handling it yourself, a few questions sharpen the decision:

  • Is the public adjuster licensed in my state, and is the fee within any legal cap?
  • What specifically will they do that I can't do myself?
  • How clear is my damage and how reasonable is the insurer's offer so far?
  • How much time and stress is the do-it-yourself path realistically going to cost me?

There's no universally correct choice — only the one that fits your claim, your state's rules, and your bandwidth.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between a public adjuster and the insurance company's adjuster?

A public adjuster is hired by and represents you, the policyholder. The company or staff adjuster works for your insurer, and an independent adjuster is hired by the insurer to inspect on its behalf. Only the public adjuster is on your side of the claim.

How much does a public adjuster charge?

Typically a percentage of the amount you recover, paid on contingency. The percentage varies and several states cap it, particularly for disaster-related claims. Since the fee comes out of your settlement, weigh it against how much their involvement is likely to increase the payout.

Can my roofer negotiate my insurance claim for me?

Generally no. In most states, negotiating a claim for a homeowner is a licensed public-adjusting activity that a contractor isn't permitted to perform just because they're doing the repair. Be wary of anyone offering to "handle your claim" or "waive your deductible."

Do I need a public adjuster for a simple roof claim?

Often not. When the damage is clear, the documentation is solid, and the insurer's offer is reasonable, many homeowners handle a straightforward claim themselves and keep the full settlement. Larger, disputed, or underpaid claims are where people more often consider professional help.
