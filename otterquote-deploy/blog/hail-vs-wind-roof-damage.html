Hail vs. Wind Damage: How Insurers Tell the Difference

Hail and wind leave different signatures on a roof — and insurers read them differently. Here's what each looks like and why it matters.

By Dustin Stohler · June 2, 2026

← All Articles

To a homeowner, storm damage is storm damage. To an insurance adjuster, hail and wind are two distinct kinds of loss with different fingerprints, and telling them apart is part of how a claim gets evaluated. Knowing the difference helps you understand what an adjuster is looking for, describe your damage accurately, and recognize when a contractor's assessment lines up with reality. Here's how the two types of damage actually look on a roof.

What Hail Damage Looks Like

Hail strikes straight down (or at a slight angle) and impacts the surface of the shingle. Its marks tend to be scattered and random rather than directional:

  • Bruises and dents — soft spots where the granules are knocked away and the mat underneath is exposed, often round.
  • Granule loss that collects in gutters and downspouts, leaving dark, pitted circles on the shingle.
  • A random, spatter-like pattern across slopes, with matching dents on soft metals like vents, flashing, and gutters.

Adjusters frequently confirm hail by checking those soft metal surfaces and by looking for a consistent strike pattern across the roof and other exposed surfaces around the property.

What Wind Damage Looks Like

Wind works differently. Instead of impacting the surface, it lifts and pulls, so its damage is directional and concentrated where the wind hit hardest:

  • Lifted, creased, or folded shingles where the seal was broken and the shingle bent back.
  • Missing shingles entirely, often in patches along edges, ridges, and corners where uplift is strongest.
  • Torn or displaced flashing and damage that follows a clear directional line rather than a random spatter.

Because wind concentrates on the windward edges and high points, wind damage often clusters, while hail tends to be spread more evenly across the roof.

Why the Distinction Matters for a Claim

The type of peril affects how a claim is handled. Many policies carry a separate wind/hail deductible — sometimes a percentage of dwelling coverage rather than a flat amount — and the cause of loss noted on the claim can influence coverage. Damage that an adjuster attributes to age, wear, or a manufacturing issue rather than a storm event may be treated very differently from sudden hail or wind damage.

This is why documentation helps: dated photos taken after the storm, plus the storm's date, give the adjuster context and help establish that the damage came from a specific weather event rather than gradual wear.

Damage You Can't See From the Ground

Both hail and wind can cause harm that isn't visible from the driveway. Hail can fracture a shingle's mat without obvious surface loss, shortening its life and opening the door to leaks months later. Wind can break the adhesive seal between shingles without physically moving them, leaving them vulnerable to the next gust. This is why a proper roof assessment looks closely at the surface and the seals — not just whether shingles are obviously missing. A roof that looks fine from the ground can still have storm damage worth addressing.

What to Do After a Storm

A calm, orderly response serves you better than rushing:

  • Stay safe. Don't climb onto a damaged roof; assess from the ground and through photos.
  • Document promptly. Dated photos of shingles, gutters, vents, and any interior leaks, plus the storm date.
  • Get a professional assessment to understand the extent of the damage.
  • Mind the deadlines. Policies have time limits for reporting a claim, so don't wait indefinitely.
An important boundary: classifying a loss and applying your policy is the adjuster's and insurer's job. Frequently Asked Questions

How can I tell if my roof has hail or wind damage?

Hail tends to leave random, scattered dents and granule loss, with matching marks on soft metals like vents and gutters. Wind leaves directional damage — lifted, creased, or missing shingles concentrated along edges and ridges. A professional assessment confirms which you have, since some damage isn't visible from the ground.

Does it matter whether my claim is for hail or wind?

It can. Many policies have a specific wind/hail deductible, sometimes set as a percentage of dwelling coverage, and the noted cause of loss can affect how the claim is handled. Your carrier and policy determine the details.

Can a roof have storm damage that I can't see?

Yes. Hail can fracture a shingle's mat without obvious surface loss, and wind can break the seal between shingles without moving them. Both can lead to leaks later, which is why a close inspection of the surface and seals matters, not just a look for missing shingles.

How soon do I need to report storm damage?

Policies set time limits for reporting a claim, so report promptly once you've documented the damage. Waiting too long can complicate a claim. Check your policy for the specific deadline and contact your carrier with questions.
