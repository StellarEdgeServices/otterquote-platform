When NOT to File a Homeowner's Insurance Claim for Your Roof

Filing a claim isn't always the right move. Sometimes it costs more than it pays. Here's how to think it through before you call it in.

By Dustin Stohler · June 2, 2026

← All Articles

Insurance is there for genuine, sudden losses — a hailstorm that shreds your shingles, a tree through the roof. But not every roof problem belongs on a claim. In some situations, filing can leave you worse off: the payout barely clears your deductible, or the claim follows you onto future premiums. This is a general guide to the trade-offs, not advice about your specific policy. Your carrier and your declarations page have the final word.

1. The Damage Costs Less Than Your Deductible

This is the clearest case. If your deductible is $1,500 and the repair is $900, a claim pays you nothing — you'd cover the whole repair anyway — and you've still put a claim on your record. Get a contractor estimate first. When the repair cost is at or below your deductible, filing usually makes no financial sense.

Mind percentage deductibles: many policies use a wind/hail deductible set as a percentage of your dwelling coverage, not a flat dollar amount. On a higher-value home, that can be far larger than you expect — check your declarations page before assuming.

2. The Repair Is Just Above the Deductible

Even when a claim would pay something, a small net payout may not be worth it. If the repair is $2,000 against a $1,500 deductible, the claim nets you about $500 — and a claim on your history can influence future premiums for years. Weigh that modest recovery against the possibility of higher renewals and decide whether it's worth trading.

3. The Damage Is From Wear, Age, or Neglect

Homeowner's policies cover sudden, accidental damage — not wear and tear. A roof failing from age, curling shingles at end of life, or problems traced to deferred maintenance are generally excluded. Filing a claim that's clearly maintenance is likely to be denied, and a denied claim still appears on your record. If the issue is age rather than an event, repair or replacement on your own dime is usually the realistic path.

4. You've Filed Recently

Claim history matters. Multiple claims in a short window can affect your premium and, in some cases, your insurability. If you've recently filed, a small additional claim deserves extra scrutiny — the long-term cost of another mark on your record can outweigh a modest payout. This is a judgment call worth discussing directly with your agent.

When Filing Usually Does Make Sense

The flip side matters too. A claim is often clearly worth it when the loss is sudden and substantial:

  • Major storm or hail damage well above your deductible.
  • A tree or debris through the roof causing structural and interior damage.
  • Sudden, accidental damage that's plainly a covered peril rather than wear.

In these cases the payout typically dwarfs the deductible and the premium consideration, and filing is the sensible move. The "don't file" guidance above is about the gray-zone cases, not genuine disasters.

Document the Damage Either Way

Whether or not you end up filing, document the damage thoroughly the moment it's safe to do so. Take dated photos from the ground, note when the storm hit, and keep any contractor estimates together in one place. There are two reasons this matters even if you lean toward not filing. First, you may discover hidden damage — an interior leak that surfaces weeks later — that changes the math and makes a claim worthwhile after all, and a contemporaneous record supports it. Second, good documentation protects you against a contractor who later exaggerates the damage to inflate a job. A clear, dated record of your roof's condition is useful no matter which path you choose.

How to Decide Without Guessing

A short sequence keeps the decision grounded:

  • Get a real estimate. You can't compare anything to your deductible until you know the repair cost.
  • Find your deductible — including any percentage-based wind/hail deductible — on your declarations page.
  • Ask your agent how a claim might affect your specific premium and record. Asking a question is not the same as filing.
  • Compare the net payout against the long-term cost before deciding.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will filing a roof claim raise my premium?

It can. Insurers weigh claim history when setting premiums, and multiple recent claims tend to have more effect than a single large one. The impact varies by carrier, state, and your overall record, so ask your agent how a specific claim might affect your renewal before you file.

Should I file if the repair barely exceeds my deductible?

Often it's not worth it. A small net payout has to be weighed against a claim on your record and possible premium effects. Get the estimate, subtract your deductible, and compare what's left against the long-term cost before deciding.

Does an insurance claim stay on my record even if it's denied?

Generally yes. Filing creates a record regardless of the outcome, and claim-history databases are used by insurers. That's one reason a likely-to-be-denied maintenance claim usually isn't worth filing.

What's a percentage deductible?

Some policies set the wind or hail deductible as a percentage of your dwelling coverage rather than a flat amount. For example, 2% of $300,000 in coverage is a $6,000 deductible. Check your declarations page, because a percentage deductible can be much larger than a typical flat one.
