7 Red Flags in a Roofing Contractor Estimate

The lowest number on the page is not the same as the best estimate. Here are seven warning signs that tell you to slow down before you sign.

By Dustin Stohler · June 2, 2026

← All Articles

A roofing estimate is a sales document and a scope of work at the same time. A good one tells you exactly what you're buying: which materials, how much of them, what's included, and what isn't. A bad one hides the gaps until the crew is on your roof and the "extras" start adding up. You don't need to be a roofer to spot the difference — you just need to know which seven things to look for. Here they are.

1. No Itemized Materials or Quantities

A real estimate lists what's going on your roof: the shingle brand and line, the number of squares, underlayment, ice-and-water shield, drip edge, flashing, ridge vent, and the count of each. A one-line "Tear off and replace roof — $11,500" tells you nothing. If you can't see the quantities, you can't compare two bids, and you can't tell whether a "savings" came from a lower price or a smaller scope.

Ask for it in writing: "Please itemize materials, quantities, and labor separately." A contractor who builds honest estimates will already have this; one who resists is telling you something.

2. A Price That's Far Below Every Other Bid

When one number comes in dramatically under the others — not 10% lower, but 35% or 40% lower — that gap is rarely a gift. It usually means one of a few things: the scope is smaller than it looks, the materials are a cheaper grade, the crew is uninsured, or the low number is a foot in the door that grows once work begins.

This is why a single quote is risky and why comparing several side by side is the homeowner's best protection. The market price for a job is the cluster the legitimate bids fall into — an outlier on either end deserves an explanation.

3. No License or Proof of Insurance on the Document

A legitimate estimate references the contractor's license where one is required and is backed by current general-liability and workers'-compensation coverage. If a roofer is uninsured and a worker is hurt on your property, you can be exposed. "Trust me, we're covered" is not proof.

  • Ask for a Certificate of Insurance (COI) naming the coverage and dates — ideally sent directly from the insurer or agent.
  • Verify the license with your state or local licensing body, not just the logo on the truck.

4. Large Upfront Deposits or Cash-Only Terms

Materials require a reasonable deposit, but a demand for half the job up front — or full payment before any work — is a red flag. So is "cash only." Legitimate contractors accept traceable payment and tie later payments to milestones (materials delivered, tear-off complete, final inspection passed).

The pattern to avoid is simple: a big deposit, then delays, then a crew that never returns. A staged payment schedule written into the contract protects you because the contractor only gets paid as the work actually progresses.

5. Pressure to Sign Today

"This price is only good if you sign right now" is a sales tactic, not a real constraint. Material costs don't swing hour to hour, and a roof that has lasted through last week can wait two more days for you to compare bids. High-pressure closing is especially common after a storm, when crews flood a neighborhood and bank on urgency overriding judgment.

A contractor confident in the value of their work will give you time to read the estimate and check references. The ones who can't afford for you to look closely are the ones pushing hardest to sign.

6. Vague or Missing Warranty Terms

There are two warranties on every roof: the manufacturer's warranty on the materials and the contractor's workmanship warranty on the installation. A solid estimate names both — the shingle line and its warranty tier, and the length of the installer's labor guarantee. "Lifetime warranty" with no paper behind it means nothing.

Workmanship warranties matter more than most homeowners realize, because the majority of leaks trace back to installation, not material defects. If the workmanship guarantee is missing or suspiciously short, ask why before you sign.

7. "We'll Handle Your Insurance Claim for You"

Be cautious with any contractor who offers to manage your insurance claim, negotiate with your adjuster, or "make sure you pay nothing but your deductible." Negotiating a claim on a homeowner's behalf can cross into public-adjusting, which is a licensed activity in most states — and "we'll waive your deductible" can be insurance fraud.

How to Read Past the Bottom Line

Once you've ruled out the obvious red flags, compare estimates on equal footing. Line the bids up and check that each one covers the same scope: same number of squares, same underlayment, same flashing and ventilation work. A bid that looks cheaper often is cheaper because it quietly leaves something out — reused flashing instead of new, no ice-and-water shield, or a thinner underlayment.

When the scopes match, the remaining price difference reflects real factors: material grade, crew experience, warranty length, and overhead. That's a decision you can make with open eyes — which is the entire point of getting more than one estimate.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many roofing estimates should I get?

Three is a common, sensible number. It's enough to establish the market range for your job and to spot an outlier — whether suspiciously low or unusually high — without turning the process into a part-time job. The goal is to compare like-for-like scope, not just to collect numbers.

Is the lowest roofing bid ever the right choice?

It can be — if the scope, materials, insurance, and warranty all match the other bids. The lowest price is only a problem when it's low because something was left out. Confirm the estimates cover the same work before you treat price as the deciding factor.

What deposit is normal for a roof replacement?

A modest deposit to cover materials is standard; demands for half or all of the job up front are not. Look for a staged payment schedule tied to milestones, and prefer traceable payment methods over cash-only terms.

Should I use a contractor who offers to handle my insurance claim?

Be careful. Negotiating a claim for a homeowner can require a public-adjuster license, and offers to "waive your deductible" may be fraud. A contractor's job is to document damage and write a clear estimate. For the claim itself, work with your carrier or a licensed professional.
