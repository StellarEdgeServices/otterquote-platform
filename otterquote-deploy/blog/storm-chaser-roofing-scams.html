Storm Chaser Roofing Scams: How to Spot One

After every big storm, the door-knocks start. Some are legitimate roofers; some are here for one season. Here's how to tell them apart.

By Dustin Stohler · June 2, 2026

← All Articles

"Storm chaser" is the industry term for an out-of-town crew that follows severe weather, blankets a damaged neighborhood with door-knocks, signs up as many roofs as possible, and moves on. Not every door-knocker is a scammer — plenty of honest roofers canvass after storms — but the pattern attracts bad actors, and the homeowners who get burned usually missed the same handful of warning signs. Here's what to watch for and how to protect yourself.

Why Storm Chasers Target You After a Storm

The conditions after a major storm are ideal for a fast, high-pressure operation: lots of real damage, anxious homeowners, and insurance money in motion. A crew can knock dozens of doors a day, collect signatures and deposits, and either do rushed work or disappear entirely — with no local presence to hold them accountable months later when a problem surfaces. Speed and volume are the business model, which is exactly why their tactics feel rushed.

The Warning Signs

No single item proves a scam, but several together should make you slow down:

  • Out-of-state plates and temporary contact info. A magnetic door-sign, a brand-new local number, or no permanent address are all reasons to dig deeper.
  • Pressure to sign immediately — especially a contract that authorizes them to deal with your insurer.
  • "We'll waive your deductible" or "the roof is free." Waiving a deductible is illegal in many states and can be insurance fraud.
  • A large cash deposit up front, then delays.
  • Found damage you can't see and a refusal to show you photos or let you verify it.
  • No verifiable license or insurance, or evasiveness when you ask.
The deductible red line: any offer to "eat" or "waive" your deductible is a bright warning sign. It's not a discount — it usually means inflating the claim to the insurer, which is fraud you could be drawn into.

The "Free Inspection" That Isn't

A common opener is the free roof inspection. Sometimes it's genuine. The scam version has the inspector go up alone and come down with photos of "major damage" — occasionally damage they caused themselves to manufacture a claim. Protect yourself by never authorizing work based solely on a stranger's photos, and by getting an independent assessment before you sign anything. A legitimate inspector is happy to walk you through what they found.

How to Protect Yourself

A few habits defuse almost every storm-chaser tactic:

  • Never sign on the first visit. Real damage can wait a couple of days for you to think and compare.
  • Verify license and insurance independently — through your state or local licensing body, not the flyer.
  • Get more than one estimate so a single high-pressure pitch isn't your only data point.
  • Refuse deductible-waiver offers outright.
  • Keep deposits reasonable and pay through traceable methods tied to milestones.
  • Read the contract, especially any clause letting the contractor act on your behalf with your insurer.
The single best defense: control the process instead of reacting to it. When you choose who bids and compare several estimates calmly, the urgency a storm chaser depends on simply evaporates.

If You've Already Signed With One

If you signed a contract under pressure and now have doubts, don't panic. Many states give consumers a short right-to-cancel window for contracts signed at home — often three business days — and require the contractor to disclose it. Read your contract for the cancellation terms and act quickly if you're within the window. Send any cancellation in writing and keep a copy. If you paid a deposit, request it back per the contract's terms. Going forward, hold off on any further payment until you've independently verified the contractor's license and insurance and, ideally, compared their price against a second estimate. The earlier you slow the process down, the more options you keep.

A Note on Your Insurance Claim

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a storm chaser in roofing?

It's an out-of-area crew that follows severe storms, canvasses damaged neighborhoods for fast sign-ups, and moves on. Some do legitimate work, but the model rewards speed and volume over accountability, which is why scams cluster around it.

Is it illegal for a roofer to waive my insurance deductible?

In many states, yes. "Waiving" a deductible typically means the contractor inflates the insurance claim to cover it, which is fraud. Treat any deductible-waiver offer as a serious warning sign, regardless of how it's framed.

Should I let a door-knocking roofer inspect my roof?

You can, but don't authorize work or sign anything based only on their photos. Never let an unsupervised stranger's findings drive a contract. Get an independent assessment and a second estimate before committing.

How do I verify a roofer is legitimate?

Confirm their license and insurance through your state or local licensing body rather than the flyer or truck logo, look for a permanent local presence, and get more than one written estimate. Legitimate contractors welcome the scrutiny.
