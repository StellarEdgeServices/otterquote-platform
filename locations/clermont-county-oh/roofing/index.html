The southwest Ohio climate and your roofing

NOAA's Ohio state climate summary says a prolonged heat wave affected the Ohio River Valley from July 17 to 24, 2011. It also says high temperatures combined with high humidity can create dangerous heat index values. The summary names extreme heat as a particular concern for the state's urban areas. Clermont County is in the Southwest division of NOAA's Ohio climate divisions.

Roofing issues to look for after a storm

  • Impact damage that hides from the ground — hail strikes are hard to see without getting on the roof, which is why documentation matters.
  • Flashing and penetration damage — check chimneys, vents, and valleys, where post-storm leaks can start.
  • Ventilation and code items — older roofs may need code-related upgrades; ask your contractor and your building department how local code items are handled.
  • Decking and underlayment issues — discovered only at tear-off, and can be a source of change orders worth understanding in advance.
  • Wind-lifted and creased shingles — broken seal strips let later storms drive rain under the roof surface.

What to expect locally

After a storm in Clermont County, the useful work is slow: getting damage documented, raising questions with your insurer, and having the repair scoped carefully. Many homeowners find it helps to slow the process down at the start — documenting damage before tarps and repairs change the evidence, reading their policy before the first phone call, and getting more than one written bid before signing anything.

Be appropriately skeptical of anyone who shows up unsolicited after a storm, pressures you to sign paperwork on the spot, or quotes a price without getting on the roof or examining the damage up close. Storm-chasing crews can show up after any major storm, and one practical defense is unhurried, written bids that you can check against each other.

Season and timing

Post-storm weeks can be congested, and quotes gathered in a hurry may be harder to compare; the weeks after the rush can be a calmer time to review bids. Whatever the calendar says, the sequence stays the same: document first, understand your policy second, compare any written bids you receive third — and take your time rather than rushing to agree with the first person who knocks on the door.

Storm reports NOAA holds for Clermont County

Hail has fewer reports than thunderstorm wind. NOAA's Storm Events archive holds 28 hail reports for Clermont County. The reports run from April 26, 2016 through May 2, 2025, covering 17 different days. Twelve of the reports, from nine dates, measured an inch or more. Only 2021 passes without a hail report; 2026's file lists none. The largest stones, 1.50 inches, were reported twice: at Bethel on April 8, 2020, and at Felicity on April 1, 2024.

April 8, 2020 has more hail reports than any other date, five. Bethel reported 1.50 inches and 1.00 inch, Williamsburg 1.00, Laurel and New Richmond 0.75 each. The 1.50-inch Bethel report is stamped 2105 EST, 10:05 p.m. once daylight-saving time is applied. NOAA's column guide describes its clock fields as usually standard time, and every Clermont report carries EST-5, so times below are printed as NOAA has them, followed by the daylight-saving equivalent (EDT). For the February and early-March dates, clocks had not yet changed; the daylight reading there is arithmetic only. By month, hail stacks up in spring: April has twelve reports, May seven, March five, then July three and February one.

Thunderstorm wind has 142 reports across 71 dates, from March 31, 2016 through June 18, 2026. NOAA labels every one an estimated gust, none a measured one. Month by month, April is highest at 35 reports spanning 13 dates. The next three are July with 22 reports (11 dates), March with 19 (8 dates) and August with 18 (12 dates). 2020 had the most wind reports of any year (32, on 10 dates) and 2024 the fewest of any full year (4, on 4 dates). Of the 32 reports in 2020, 19 come from April 8, the most for any date; 18 of the 19 bear stamps from 2137 EST to 2159 EST, meaning 10:37 to 10:59 p.m. once daylight-saving time is applied.

The top estimate, 78 knots, appears six times: five Day Hgts entries dated April 8, 2020, plus Williamsburg's single entry of March 3, 2023. One Day Hgts entry, stamped 2142 EST (10:42 PM EDT), carries this narrative: "Numerous trees were snapped or knocked down, one onto a house." The Williamsburg entry, stamped 1711 EST (6:11 PM EDT), describes outbuildings tipped over and roofing material blown off, adds that "metal dugout awnings were destroyed", and estimates winds around 90 miles per hour at Santa Barbara Drive and the athletic fields.

Two more wind types are kept under a forecast zone, not a town, and here the zone is named CLERMONT. NWS Directive 10-1605 sets both apart as non-convective. High Wind shows seven dates (April 3, 2016 through March 13, 2026), all with NOAA's estimate at 50 knots. Strong Wind appears on four: February 19, 2016, November 18, 2017, November 15, 2020, then March 13, 2026, with readings of 43, 43, 48 and 43 knots, the first and last measured.

The tornado ledger holds eight entries on six dates: three EF0, four EF1, a single EF2. The earliest came March 1, 2017 at 0338 EST (4:38 AM EDT), an EF1 traveling from Blairsville toward a NOAA point labeled Amelia Sky Hills Arp, 6.39 miles in length and 150 yards across. Its narrative tells of a home whose roof was "completely lifted off and displaced into the backyard." An EF1 between Moscow and Felicity followed on February 25, 2018, stamped 0003 EST (1:03 AM EDT), where two barns suffered "partial roof removal."

The lone EF2 came July 6, 2022, from Goshen toward Modest, stamped 1405 EST (3:05 PM EDT). NOAA's fields give it 4.81 miles of path, a width of 500 yards and 2 direct injuries. In its narrative "An insurance agency completely lost its roof", and the tornado went on "causing at least six more homes to completely lose their roofs." A weaker EF1 from Branch Hill to Epworth Hgts, stamped 1357 EST (2:57 PM EDT), preceded it by eight minutes.

The Census view of local housing

The county has 88,470 housing units in the Census Bureau's 2020 to 2024 five-year American Community Survey (table DP04): 84,955 occupied and 3,515 vacant (4.0 percent). Occupants own 62,567 of the homes in use (73.6 percent) and rent the other 22,388 (26.4 percent). Detached houses make up 61,870 units (69.9 percent) and mobile homes 5,244 (5.9 percent).

The three largest build decades are the 1990s with 16,940 units (19.1 percent), the 1970s with 13,794 (15.6 percent) and the 2000s with 13,674 (15.5 percent). Before 1980, 36,432 units (41.2 percent) were built; in 2000 or later, 23,332 (26.4 percent). Separately, B25035 gives 1987 as the median year of construction, margin of error 2. Bulk counts say little about one house; check the paperwork for yours.

Who issues the roofing permit here

The county's permit office is Permit Central/Building Inspection, 2275 Bauer Road, Batavia, telephone 513-732-7213. Its site lists lobby hours of 8:00am to 4:00pm and business hours of 8:00am to 4:30pm on weekdays. The department's question-and-answer page opens its project list with "A permit is required for the following projects:" and that list includes "Roofing" along with "Structural Repairs". The 2026 General Permit Application likewise offers a "Roof Replacement" box among its project types. That same Q-and-A page states a plan-review goal for residential work of "10 to 15 business days", and says inspectors are typically in the office from 8:00 a.m. to 9:00 a.m. and generally around 3:00 p.m. until 4:00 p.m., Monday through Friday.

Two further county sheets bear on roofs. An application checklist asks residential applicants for a "Zoning Permit/Letter of Approval from Township/Village", which makes the local zoning desk an early stop. A separate sheet on exempt work, dated 2019 on its face, says the department "does not have authority from the State of Ohio to Approve or Inspect any Building, Mechanical, Electric or Fire Protection work" that is exempt from approval, and it names manufactured homes among the two most common exclusions. In a county with 5,244 mobile homes, the sheet deserves a read before anyone assumes a permit is or is not needed.

Two cities publish their own permit information. Milford's page on building permits identifies National Inspection Corporation, 888-433-4642, as the body monitoring building law there, and says applications "usually take 5-15 business days to process." Helpful Links on the county's site names Loveland as well, and Loveland points applicants to its building official at (513) 707-1447. Roofing goes unmentioned on both city pages, so put the question to whichever office covers your street.

On state licensing, section 4740.01 of the Revised Code defines the licenses of Ohio's construction board by five trades: heating, ventilating and air conditioning, refrigeration, electrical, plumbing and hydronics. Roofing sits outside those five. Across the Permit Central site's pages and forms opened for this research, no roofer registration rule appears.

A short list before bids go out

- Your insurer's written estimate, if one has been issued, kept as a copy each recipient can read.

- Which office covers your address: Permit Central, Milford, Loveland, or the zoning desk for your parcel.

- A line stating whether the house is site-built or manufactured; the exempt sheet singles out manufactured homes.

- A weekday morning or mid-afternoon window for phoning the county's inspectors.
