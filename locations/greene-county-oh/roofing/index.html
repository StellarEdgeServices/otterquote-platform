Roofing issues to look for after a storm

  • Flashing and penetration damage — check chimneys, vents, and valleys, where post-storm leaks can start.
  • Hail bruising and granule loss — impact marks that shorten shingle life even when no leak appears immediately.
  • Slow leaks after a storm — minor damage can go unnoticed until water stains show indoors.
  • Partial-slope damage — storms can damage one or two elevations, raising repair-versus-replace questions that comparing bids can help you think through.
  • Ventilation and code items — older roofs may need code-related upgrades; ask your contractor and your building department how local code items are handled.

What to expect locally

The practical sequence for Greene County homeowners after storm damage: document everything with photos before any cleanup, review your policy and ask your insurer how to report damage, and gather written repair bids so you can compare scope and pricing side by side. Nothing in that sequence requires committing to a contractor early — you can keep your options open while you review any written bids you receive.

Local demand can also move in waves. After a widely publicized hail event, contractors across a region may get busy at once. If you receive more than one written bid, comparing them gives you a way to check pricing when demand spikes, and can surface scope differences — what one bidder saw that another missed — before the work starts rather than after.

Season and timing

Timing matters in Greene County. The weeks after a storm can be busy for adjusters and contractors. Documentation is the part that does not depend on the calendar: photograph damage once it is safe and note the date for your adjuster. They span fourteen separate days, starting April 26, 2016 and ending April 4, 2025. 28 rows give a size of 1.00 inch or bigger. Neither 2018 nor 2022 contains hail, and the unfinished 2026 file has none either. Every Greene row reads EST-5, while NOAA's format guide describes the time field as "usually in LST". Each clock reading that follows is therefore the raw stamp, then what a daylight clock (EDT) would have shown; for dates before March's changeover, that second reading is arithmetic alone.

Whose permit for a new roof? Three desks, two answers

Greene County Building Regulation (667 Dayton-Xenia Rd., Xenia; (937) 562-7420) is open 7:30 am to 4:00 pm Monday through Friday. It says it excludes six places from its service: Fairborn, Xenia, Bowersville, Clifton, Cedarville and Yellow Springs, plus federal and state property. Beavercreek's FAQ states "The City of Beavercreek only issues zoning permits" and sends building permits to the county.

That office's own "Shingle Roof Requirements" sheet, which carries no printed date, demands a permit if rafters, trusses or sheathing need structural repair. A tear-off, or a re-roof with no structural repair, it says, needs none, though Ohio's residential or building code still governs the job, with the manufacturer's specification. The sheet calls "ice shield" "required on new and re-roofing projects" and bars a "recover" when two or more layers are already down.

Xenia's sheet, "Which Permits Do I Need?" (a PDF made in December 2024), covers it differently. Its row for building alterations names "roof replacement" and lists Zoning and Building, while a note carves interior renovation and roof replacement out of zoning. Xenia's building line is 937.433.4642, which the sheet ties to an outside inspection company. Fairborn's inspection office, (937) 754-3050, runs an online portal for permits "as well as contractor registration/renewal"; neither Fairborn's nor Beavercreek's pages opened here mention roofing, so phone the office for your address.

Under R.C. 4740.01, the construction industry licensing board of Ohio issues licenses in hydronics, plumbing, electrical, refrigeration, and heating, ventilating and air conditioning. None of those five trades is roofing.

Gather these first

- The street address, to tell which desk covers it: the county, Xenia, Fairborn or a village.

- A note on rafter, truss or sheathing repair, since the sheet draws its permit line there.

- Number of older layers under the top roofing.

- Ice shield, plus drip edge: is each named in the bids?

- Your insurer's written estimate, if one exists, shown to every bidder.
