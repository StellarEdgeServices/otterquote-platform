A roof in Marion County can take hail strikes and wind uplift during a storm, and when it fails, the damage rarely stays confined to the shingles.

The central Indiana climate and your roofing

NOAA's 2022 Indiana State Climate Summary says annual average temperature varies by about 10 degrees F from north to south, that temperatures in Indiana have risen almost 1.5 degrees F since the beginning of the 20th century, and that the warming has been concentrated in winter and spring. It also says annual precipitation has been above average since 1990. Marion County is in central Indiana.

Roofing issues to look for after a storm

  • Slow leaks after a storm — minor damage can go unnoticed until water stains show indoors.
  • Flashing and penetration damage — check chimneys, vents, and valleys, where post-storm leaks can start.
  • Hail bruising and granule loss — impact marks that shorten shingle life even when no leak appears immediately.
  • Wind-lifted and creased shingles — broken seal strips let later storms drive rain under the roof surface.
  • Ventilation and code items — older roofs may need code-related upgrades; ask your contractor and your building department how local code items are handled.

What to expect locally

After a storm in Marion County, the useful work is slow: getting damage documented, raising questions with your insurer, and having the repair scoped carefully. Many homeowners find it helps to slow the process down at the start — documenting damage before tarps and repairs change the evidence, reading their policy before the first phone call, and getting more than one written bid before signing anything.

Local demand can also move in waves. After a widely publicized hail event, contractors across a region may get busy at once. If you receive more than one written bid, comparing them gives you a way to check pricing when demand spikes, and can surface scope differences — what one bidder saw that another missed — before the work starts rather than after.

Season and timing

Timing matters in Marion County. The weeks after a storm can be busy for adjusters and contractors. Documentation is the part that does not depend on the calendar: photograph damage once it is safe and note the date for your adjuster. Ask your insurer whether your policy sets a deadline for reporting damage.

Hail, wind and tornado entries in NOAA's file

Open the Storm Events bulk files that NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information publishes for 2016 through 2026, keep the rows filed under Marion County, Indiana, and the result is 260 entries dated February 2016 through June 2026. Hail accounts for 66 of them, on 34 separate days. Forty-four of the hail entries, on 21 of those days, give a stone diameter of 1.00 inch or more. The two largest figures are 2.00 inches, one filed under Wanamaker on May 1, 2016 and one under Castleton on June 18, 2021. That June evening also carries two 1.75-inch entries, filed under Oaklandon and Five Points. The 2026 file used here is the one NOAA created on September 18, 2026; NOAA says it updates the current year's file each month, so the 2026 counts can change. The counts here leave out 48 more rows that NOAA files under forecast zones named Marion, such as High Wind and winter weather entries.

Hail in this file is not confined to the summer months. March holds more hail entries than any other month, 20 of them on 7 days, and 8 of the 20 share one date: March 27, 2016. NOAA's episode narrative for that date says thunderstorms developed "in warm and unstable air ahead of a cold front during the afternoon of Easter." The largest stones that day, 1.50 inches, are filed under Beech Grove and Homecroft, and the Homecroft entry reads "Ping pong sized hail was observed near Southport High School." After March come July with 12 entries on 6 days, then May and June with 11 each.

The wind entries outnumber the hail entries. NOAA files 152 thunderstorm wind entries for the county on 66 days; 39 of them give a measured gust and the other 113 an estimated one. June has the most, 38 entries on 12 days, followed by April with 25 and August with 23. June 18, 2025 is the busiest date at 14 entries, ahead of June 15, 2016 at 9 and both May 16, 2019 and April 2, 2025 at 8. Counted by year, 2025 is heaviest for wind, with 31 entries on 8 days. The highest thunderstorm gust in the county's file belongs to April 19, 2025: "An 84 mph thunderstorm wind gust was measured at the IND ASOS," which the episode narrative calls "the second strongest wind gust on record for the station."

What the wind did to buildings shows up in a handful of narratives. One places a roof blown off in the Sunshine Gardens area on April 8, 2020. Another, dated April 17, 2024, reads "Roof damage caused by thunderstorm wind gusts reported across Lawrence." On August 1, 2022 an entry describes five homes damaged by fallen trees and limbs in the Heatherwood Estates subdivision, and says some of those trees "went through the roofs." A May 16, 2019 entry from Mickleyville records "damage to siding of a home along the peak of the roof line," and an entry from Speedway on July 27, 2020 says two windows and a window frame were blown in. The newest roof reference is June 11, 2026, from the Acton area: "roof damage on Knapp and Mitthoeffer Roads."

Only three tornado entries sit in the file, on two days. An EF0 filed under Woodruff Place on August 24, 2016 blew windows out of a home near 25th and LaSalle Streets, and a building on Stanton Street sustained roof damage. On June 15, 2019 an EF1 began southwest of Beech Grove with winds NOAA puts at 100 mph; the narrative lists roof damage to a school, an apartment complex and a house, and says more than 20 classrooms at Beech Grove High School were damaged or destroyed. An EF0 near Five Points the same evening damaged outbuildings.

These are entries, not storms. A single afternoon can produce a dozen or more of them, and an entry exists only where someone reported or measured something. A quiet stretch in the file says nothing about whether a particular roof was struck, and an entry from a neighborhood a few miles away says little about the roof over your head. Housing stock

The Census Bureau's American Community Survey 5-year estimates for 2020 to 2024, table DP04, count 441,040 housing units in Marion County, 400,432 of them occupied. Owners live in 226,441 of the occupied units (56.5 percent) and renters in 173,991 (43.5 percent). Detached single-unit houses are the most common type at 261,039 units (59.2 percent), and buildings with 20 or more units hold 38,715 (8.8 percent).

By year built, the table places 66,271 units (15.0 percent) in 1939 or earlier, 25,337 in the 1940s, 53,751 in the 1950s, 59,103 in the 1960s and 54,891 in the 1970s. Those five bands add up to 259,353 units, or 58.8 percent of the total, built before 1980. The 1960s is the largest single-decade band at 13.4 percent. At the other end, 29,069 units (6.6 percent) date from 2010 to 2019. A house's build year is not its roof's age, since roofs get replaced, so ask when yours was last redone and whether paperwork exists.

Permits and local rules

The Department of Business and Neighborhood Services for the City of Indianapolis and Marion County publishes a Homeowner's Building Permit Guide. The copy read for this page carries no revision date. It says the department "is located at 1200 S Madison Ave, Indianapolis, Suite 100," directs residential permit questions to (317) 327-8700, and states: "Any construction requiring a building permit also requires that the applicant either be a licensed contractor in Marion county, or the property owner."

On roofs the guide speaks in two places. Under Common Projects it says: "Re-roofing and replacing siding will require a permit." It adds that a re-roofing permit may be obtained online through its Citizen's Access Portal or in person at the department. Under Work Exempt from Structural Permitting it lists "Replacement of an existing roof, if performed by a listed contractor that complies with the notice and posting requirements of section 536-216," unless the work changes the roof configuration, changes the covering in a way that would "increase the dead load," replaces the framing that supports the roof, "(e.g., replacement of a rafter or more than 128 feet of decking)," or uses "heat-applied roofing material." The same list adds that an owner-occupant "may affix without permit a layer of replacement shingles to a single layer of existing shingles" on stated conditions, and it warns that "an Improvement Location Permit could be required depending on scope." The guide does not say how the exemption relates to the re-roofing permit passage, so ask the department which applies at your address.

Its Adopted Codes section lists a "2020 International Residential Code (IRC)" and prints a Table 301.2(1) for the city: ground snow load 20, wind speed 115 (MPH), weathering "Severe," frost line depth 30 inches, winter design temperature 2 degrees F, and "No" under ice shield underlayment required. The guide's introduction says its references "use the 2020 Indiana Residential Code." For hired work, the guide says "the contractor must pull all required permits using her or his license," so that "the homeowner is protected from any violations or fees."

Rules change at some city lines inside the county. Lawrence, which appears in the county's NOAA file, has its own Department of Public Works roofing handout. It says "Building permits are NOT required except under the following conditions," and the list includes "25% or more of the decking is being replace OR," "After a natural disaster OR," a change in roof configuration, and a change in the type of roof covering "that would increase the dead load on the structure." It also says "All contractors engaged in roofing work must be registered in the City of Lawrence" and limits new roofing over an existing roof to "a total of two layers." The handout calls itself "intended only as a guide," based in part on the 2020 Indiana Residential Code, so confirm with the department at 317-545-8787.

What to settle before you ask for bids

- Which office issues roof permits at your address: the Department of Business and Neighborhood Services, or the building office of a city such as Lawrence.

- Whether each written bid names who pulls the permit, since the guide says the party doing the work should.

- The date of the most recent storm to reach your street, with photos taken afterward.

- The year your roof was last replaced, if any record exists.

Frequently asked questions

Should I repair or replace after partial-slope damage?

Shingle availability and the age of the roof both come into it; your insurer can tell you how your policy addresses matching. Written bids that price both paths let you compare the two side by side.

Do I need to be home when my contractor or insurer inspects the roof?

For the exterior portion, usually not — but being present means you see the documented damage yourself and can ask questions of whoever inspects the roof.
