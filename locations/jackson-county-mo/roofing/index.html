A severe storm in Jackson County can leave roofs with the full menu of problems: hail bruising, wind-lifted shingles, damaged flashing, and the slow leaks that follow. Getting more than one written bid is one way to understand what a repair may cost.

The Northwest Prairie climate and your roofing

Jackson County is in the Northwest Prairie, which is division 1 of NOAA's six Missouri climate divisions. The Missouri Climate Center says that Missouri "has a continental type of climate marked by strong seasonality" and that "The basic gradient for most climatic characteristics is along a line diagonally crossing the state from northwest to southeast." It puts mean annual precipitation at "a low of 34 inches in the northwest to a high of 50 inches in the southeast," and says that in northwestern Missouri "June precipitation, for example, averages five times greater than January precipitation."

Roofing issues to look for after a storm

  • Flashing and penetration damage — check chimneys, vents, and valleys, where post-storm leaks can start.
  • Slow leaks after a storm — minor damage can go unnoticed until water stains show indoors.
  • Partial-slope damage — storms can damage one or two elevations, raising repair-versus-replace questions that comparing bids can help you think through.
  • Wind-lifted and creased shingles — broken seal strips let later storms drive rain under the roof surface.
  • Hail bruising and granule loss — impact marks that shorten shingle life even when no leak appears immediately.

What to expect locally

The practical sequence for Jackson County homeowners after storm damage: document everything with photos before any cleanup, review your policy and ask your insurer how to report damage, and gather written repair bids so you can compare scope and pricing side by side. Nothing in that sequence requires committing to a contractor early — you can keep your options open while you review any written bids you receive.

Local demand can also move in waves. After a widely publicized hail event, contractors across a region may get busy at once. If you receive more than one written bid, comparing them gives you a way to check pricing when demand spikes, and can surface scope differences — what one bidder saw that another missed — before the work starts rather than after.

Season and timing

Timing is worth planning. The weeks after a storm can be busy for adjusters and contractors, and winter weather can limit exterior work. Document the damage first, then take the time you need to compare any written bids you receive.

Roof bids are easier to compare once they are lined up on the same items. Begin with scope. Does the bid cover every slope or only the damaged one? Does it include the garage and any porch roof? Does it list the tear-off, the disposal of old shingles and the cleanup with a magnet for stray nails? Write down the answers beside each total.

Pay attention to what lies between the shingles and the wood. Ask for the name of the underlayment, whether a waterproof membrane is placed along the eaves and in the valleys, and how the bid treats the ridge, the hips and the vents. A roof that vents poorly can trap heat and moisture in the attic, so ask whether intake and exhaust openings are being added, cleared, or left as they are.

Ask for the shingle by brand, line, color and style, and for the warranty documents in writing. Architectural and three-tab shingles are not the same product, and the bid should not leave you guessing which is meant. Ask how many nails are used per shingle and where they go, and ask to see the manufacturer's installation sheet. If a bid's author cannot show you the sheet, that is useful to know.

Before work begins, take date-stamped photos of every slope, the gutters and the landscaping beside the house. Ask where the dumpster or trailer will sit and how the lawn will be protected. Make sure the start and end terms are written down.

One more habit pays off with any bid: circle every line that uses a vague word such as allowance, as needed, or if required, and ask what number or condition sits behind it. Vague lines are where two proposals drift apart, and a short list of clarified lines makes the final comparison far simpler. Keep the list beside the photographs you took of the slopes, the ridge and the eaves, so every question points to something you can both see.
