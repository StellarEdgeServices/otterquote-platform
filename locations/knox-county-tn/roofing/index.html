Roofing issues to look for after a storm

  • Slow leaks after a storm — minor damage can go unnoticed until water stains show indoors.
  • Wind-lifted and creased shingles — broken seal strips let later storms drive rain under the roof surface.
  • Ventilation and code items — older roofs may need code-related upgrades; ask your contractor and your building department how local code items are handled.
  • Impact damage that hides from the ground — hail strikes are hard to see without getting on the roof, which is why documentation matters.
  • Decking and underlayment issues — discovered only at tear-off, and can be a source of change orders worth understanding in advance.

What to expect locally

Homeowners in Knox County navigating a storm claim juggle three parallel tracks: the insurance process (adjuster inspection, scope, settlement), the contractor process (bids, scheduling, materials), and their own documentation. Keeping those tracks separate is the single most useful habit — your insurer decides coverage under your policy; your contractor proposes the repair scope; and written bids let you compare scope and pricing side by side.

Local demand can also move in waves. After a widely publicized hail event, contractors across a region may get busy at once. If you receive more than one written bid, comparing them gives you a way to check pricing when demand spikes, and can surface scope differences — what one bidder saw that another missed — before the work starts rather than after.

Season and timing

Timing matters in Knox County. The weeks after a storm can be busy for adjusters and contractors. Documentation is the part that does not depend on the calendar: photograph damage once it is safe and note the date for your adjuster. Ask your insurer whether your policy sets a deadline for reporting damage.

Knox County in NOAA's archive

Hail is the thin part of Knox County's record, and it is lumpy. The Storm Events Database lists 42 hail reports on 19 days between May 2016 and October 2025. Spring brings 18 of them: April and May, spread over 6 days. Then comes a September surprise, 9 reports squeezed into September 4, 2025, the heaviest single hail day in the county's record. Counted by year, 2025 leads with 16 reports on 3 days (April 10 with 6, September 4 with 9, October 21 with 1). The latest entry we pulled is dated May 20, 2026, and NOAA marks all 2026 data as preliminary.

Seven NOAA hail reports reach 1.75 inches, the top size for 2016 through 2025. The places are Farragut on May 2, 2016, where the narrative says golf-ball-size hail; near Clinton Highway and Schaad Road at Powell on March 21, 2017; West Knoxville on April 22, 2017, with a narrative adding "several locations in the surrounding vicinity"; Millertown on May 26, 2024; Solway and Robinson on April 10, 2025; and Beaver Ridge on September 4, 2025. Smaller 1.50-inch reports include Virtue, three miles west of Farragut, on the same day as the Farragut report in 2016, and Lovell on July 14, 2016.

Wind entries outnumber hail entries by more than four to one: 182 thunderstorm wind reports on 94 days between February 2016 and May 2026, according to NOAA. July (37) and August (34) lead the months, and May through August total 127 reports on 66 days. The busiest year in the NOAA record is 2023 with 31 reports, led by August 12, 2023 with 7. May 26, 2024 stands out for pairing 6 wind reports with the Millertown hail. Five reports at 61 knots on March 29, 2020, filed between 4:46 and 5:09 a.m. Eastern Standard Time under Cowards, Blue Grass, Farragut, South Knoxville and Lake Forest, come with radar-estimated times in three of their NOAA narratives. Other 61-knot entries are Copper Ridge (June 21, 2021, with radar-estimated winds near 70 mph and trees down across north Knox County), Beverly (July 30, 2024), Norwood (May 20, 2025) and Sequoyah Hills (December 18, 2025). The peak, 63 knots, was read from a home anemometer at Lovell on May 20, 2025, and May 20, 2026 closes the data with 5 more reports.

Seven more NOAA entries use the High Wind or Strong Wind labels. A 52-knot High Wind entry for September 27, 2024 cites about 100 Knox 911 calls for fallen trees during the Helene wind event, and a December 29, 2024 entry says the McGhee Tyson weather station recorded gusts above 40 mph for several hours, peaking at 51 mph at 12:49 p.m.

NOAA lists one tornado report between 2016 and May 2026, an EF2 on August 7, 2023 near Byrds Chapel. Its narrative has the tornado starting on Yarnell Road and reaching its peak width as it passed the Lovell Crossing apartment complex. Every entry above is a weather report for the county as a whole. None points to a particular home, and none forecasts a future storm.

Housing stock

Table DP04 of the Census Bureau's 2020 to 2024 American Community Survey (5-year) gives Knox County 218,661 housing units, 201,146 of them occupied. Homeowners live in 131,117 of the occupied units (65.2 percent); renters live in 70,029. By building type, 144,045 units (65.9 percent) are single-unit detached homes, 43,404 (19.8 percent) are in buildings of 5 or more units, and 7,792 (3.6 percent) are mobile homes. The survey puts median construction at 1985, with 2000 to 2009 the biggest single band (32,892 units), just ahead of 1990 to 1999 (32,641). Adding every band up to 1979 in that survey gives 94,524 units (43.2 percent), and 14,943 (6.8 percent) go back to 1939 or earlier. A general observation, not a statistic: where housing is older, more roofs have already been through one replacement.

Permits and local rules for a re-roof

The City of Knoxville's Plans Review and Inspections office, at 400 Main Street, Suite 475, is the city's permit office. Its Plans Review and Permits page lists residential permit types that include Residential Alteration/Repair, and its Fast Fact Guides page, which lists 17 guides including Residential Permit Requirements and Third-Party Inspections for IRC Residential Projects, gives a general rule that one- and two-family dwellings and townhouses need a construction permit unless the work is exempt under section R105.2 of the residential code. The city's Construction Codes page lists the 2024 International Residential Code. The same guides page, in its section on state design rules, says "A qualified registrant is required for roof replacements or reroofs of all buildings of 5,000 square feet or more or greater than two stories in height," and it exempts one- and two-family dwellings and townhouses from needing a registered architect or engineer. The pages we read do not say in plain words whether a re-roof of a house needs a permit, and they state no layer limit or ice barrier rule, so check with Plans Review and Inspections (865-215-4311) before work starts.

The Plans Review and Inspections page on knoxvilletn.gov has quick links for Permit Search, Inspection Scheduling, a Daily Inspections List and "Renew or Register to Pull a Permit."

Knox County Codes Administration sits in Suite 547 of the City County Building at the same 400 Main Street address, with a help line at 865-215-2325 and hours listed as Monday through Friday, 8:00 am to 4:30 pm. Its page directs applicants to the Knox Permit Portal and offers a Permit Inspection Lookup for inspection results and scheduled inspections. Its download list includes a Homeowner Permit Affidavit, an Affidavit of Exemption, a Schedule of Fees and a Third-Party Inspection Policy. The page does not say which addresses fall under it, so check with Knox County Codes Administration if your home may be outside the city limits.

The state layer comes from the Tennessee Board for Licensing Contractors, whose pages were read in early October 2026. Knox is fifth in its list of nine counties where residential remodeling from $3,000 up to less than $25,000 falls under a Home Improvement license, and roofing is named among the improvements. At the top end, the board's contractor page puts the threshold at "projects of $25,000 or more." tn.gov's Verify a License search is the place to check a number.

Before you ask for bids on a Knox County roof

- The insurer's written estimate, when one exists, saved for each bidder.

- Whether your building is a one- or two-family home or a townhouse, because the registrant rule above exempts those.

- The answer to which office handles your address: the city, the Town of Farragut or Knox County Codes Administration.

- Three answers from each bidder: who holds the permit, the permit number, and the Tennessee license number.
