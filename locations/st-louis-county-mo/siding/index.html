Siding issues to look for after a storm

  • Oxidation lines and chalking — complicate spot repairs on older siding and affect how a scope is written.
  • Wind-creased and detached panels — compromised locking legs that let subsequent weather work panels loose.
  • Fastener pull-through in high wind — panels that look intact but are no longer attached the way the manufacturer intended.
  • Discontinued-profile matching — a common question when only some elevations are damaged.
  • Cracked corner posts and trim — small components that drive disproportionate water damage when ignored.

What to expect locally

The practical sequence for St. Louis County homeowners after storm damage: document everything with photos before any cleanup, review your policy and ask your insurer how to report damage, and gather written repair bids so you can compare scope and pricing side by side. Nothing in that sequence requires committing to a contractor early — you can keep your options open while you review any written bids you receive.

Ask any contractor for an itemized written estimate, proof of insurance, and local references. The process can take longer after a widespread storm, when repair schedules fill up. Patience and paperwork usually serve homeowners better than speed and pressure.

Siding in St. Louis County covers a lot of ground, from brick-trimmed frame houses to fully clad two-story homes, and the paperwork you collect should show that difference. Start by deciding what you are asking for: repair of a few damaged boards, replacement of one wall, or the whole house. A bid for a single elevation is a different product from a bid for four, and a partial job raises the question of how new boards will look beside weathered ones.

Ask whoever prepares each bid to name the product, not just the category. Vinyl, fiber cement, engineered wood and aluminum each come with their own profile widths, color ranges and fastening rules, and the bid should give the manufacturer, the line, the color and the thickness or gauge. Ask whether the color is baked into the board or painted on, and whether touch-up material is left with you.

What goes under the siding deserves its own line. Look for house wrap or another water-resistive layer, flashing above windows and doors, and a plan for the corners and the bottom edge where the wall meets the foundation. If any old boards are removed, ask what will be done if rot or insects are found behind them, and whether a price for that extra work is stated in advance instead of being worked out on the day.

Walk the house with a notepad before the first visit. Note the downspouts, light fixtures, outlets, dryer vents and hose bibs that sit on the wall, since each one has to be taken off and put back or cut around. Photograph the existing siding in daylight, wall by wall. Compare bids line by line, keep the written copies.

Frequently asked questions

Who decides how matching is handled for my siding?

Your insurer decides how matching is handled under your policy, and discontinued profiles can complicate the conversation. Document the damage thoroughly and get written bids that address matching explicitly, so you can compare scope and pricing side by side.

Do all elevations get replaced if one is damaged?

Not automatically. A scope can range from single-elevation repair to full replacement; ask your adjuster how matching is handled, and consider bids that spell out both scopes.
