The Northeast Prairie climate and your roofing

St. Louis County is in the Northeast Prairie, which NOAA's climate division list uses as division 2 of Missouri's six. The Missouri Climate Center says that "mean July precipitation is greatest in northeastern Missouri, largely the result of high-intensity convectional precipitation (4.4 inches), and least in southwestern Missouri (3.2 inches)." It also says that "The metropolitan areas of St. Louis and Kansas City exert a significant and measurable effect on their climates," with temperatures "elevated in both regions by a few degrees."

Roofing issues to look for after a storm

  • Slow leaks after a storm — minor damage can go unnoticed until water stains show indoors.
  • Wind-lifted and creased shingles — broken seal strips let later storms drive rain under the roof surface.
  • Impact damage that hides from the ground — hail strikes are hard to see without getting on the roof, which is why documentation matters.
  • Hail bruising and granule loss — impact marks that shorten shingle life even when no leak appears immediately.
  • Flashing and penetration damage — check chimneys, vents, and valleys, where post-storm leaks can start.

What to expect locally

After a storm in St. Louis County, the useful work is slow: getting damage documented, raising questions with your insurer, and having the repair scoped carefully. Many homeowners find it helps to slow the process down at the start — documenting damage before tarps and repairs change the evidence, reading their policy before the first phone call, and getting more than one written bid before signing anything.

Be appropriately skeptical of anyone who shows up unsolicited after a storm, pressures you to sign paperwork on the spot, or quotes a price without getting on the roof or examining the damage up close. Storm-chasing crews can show up after any major storm, and one practical defense is unhurried, written bids that you can check against each other.

Season and timing

Storm damage in Northeast Prairie can arrive in clusters: a single hail event can affect many roofs, gutters, and siding elevations in an area at once. Comparing more than one written bid for the same scope is one way homeowners check pricing when demand spikes.

A reader in St. Louis County who is looking at an older ranch or split-level should treat the roof section of any written bid as a short list of decisions rather than one price. The first decision is whether the old shingles are stripped to bare boards or whether a new layer is laid over them. Stripping lets whoever does the work see the wood deck; an overlay does not. Neither choice is wrong by definition, but the bid should say which one is meant, and it should say what happens if soft or stained boards turn up once the old surface is gone.

Next, read down the flashing items. Brick chimneys, plumbing stacks, wall-to-roof joints and the points where two slopes meet are where water tends to find a way in, so a bid that mentions only shingles and a total leaves out the parts a homeowner most wants spelled out. Look for words such as step flashing, counter flashing, drip edge, ridge cap and pipe boots, and look for a note on whether the old metal is reused or replaced.

Then compare how each bid treats the lowest edge of the roof and the valleys. Some bids add a self-sealing membrane there, and some do not. If one bid names it and another is silent, the two totals are not measuring the same job. Do the same with underlayment, starter strips and attic airflow: intake at the eaves, exhaust near the ridge, and whether anything is blocking the soffit.

Keep photographs of the roof from the ground and, if you can do it safely, of any ceiling stains inside, dated before work starts. Ask for the shingle maker and product line by name, the color, the number of bundles, and the warranty paper that comes with them. Ask who will be on the property, what is to be done with old nails and torn-off material, and how the yard and gutters will be protected. Finally, keep every page of whatever you sign.
