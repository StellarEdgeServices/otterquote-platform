A severe storm in Lake County can leave roofs with the full menu of problems: hail bruising, wind-lifted shingles, damaged flashing, and the slow leaks that follow. Getting more than one written bid is one way to understand what a repair may cost.

The northern Indiana climate and your roofing

Lake County sits in northern Indiana. NOAA's 2022 Indiana State Climate Summary says the state's location in the interior of North America, with no mountains to the north or south, exposes it to bitterly cold Arctic air masses in winter and warm, humid air masses from the Gulf of Mexico in summer. It adds that dangerous storms can occur in every season: winter can bring snow and ice storms, and thunderstorms capable of producing floods, hail and tornadoes are common in the warmer months.

Roofing issues to look for after a storm

  • Hail bruising and granule loss — impact marks that shorten shingle life even when no leak appears immediately.
  • Partial-slope damage — storms can damage one or two elevations, raising repair-versus-replace questions that comparing bids can help you think through.
  • Flashing and penetration damage — check chimneys, vents, and valleys, where post-storm leaks can start.
  • Ventilation and code items — older roofs may need code-related upgrades; ask your contractor and your building department how local code items are handled.
  • Impact damage that hides from the ground — hail strikes are hard to see without getting on the roof, which is why documentation matters.

What to expect locally

The practical sequence for Lake County homeowners after storm damage: document everything with photos before any cleanup, review your policy and ask your insurer how to report damage, and gather written repair bids so you can compare scope and pricing side by side. Nothing in that sequence requires committing to a contractor early — you can keep your options open while you review any written bids you receive.

Be appropriately skeptical of anyone who shows up unsolicited after a storm, pressures you to sign paperwork on the spot, or quotes a price without getting on the roof or examining the damage up close. Storm-chasing crews can show up after any major storm, and one practical defense is unhurried, written bids that you can check against each other.

Season and timing

Storm damage in northern Indiana can arrive in clusters: a single hail event can affect many roofs, gutters, and siding elevations in an area at once. Comparing more than one written bid for the same scope is one way homeowners check pricing when demand spikes.

Wind first, then hail and tornadoes, in NOAA's northwest Indiana entries

NOAA's Storm Events bulk files for 2016 through 2026 hold 268 entries filed under Lake County, Indiana, running from June 2016 to June 2026. Wind dominates the file: 164 thunderstorm wind entries on 56 days, against 28 hail entries on 18 days and 22 tornado entries on 12 days. Of the wind entries, 36 give a measured gust and 128 an estimated one. March is the leading month for wind with 43 entries on 6 days, ahead of July with 36 on 12 days, June with 31 on 14 days, May with 24 on 9 days and August with 22 on 8 days. Counted by year, 2025 leads with 46 entries on 9 days. The 2026 file used here is the one NOAA created on September 18, 2026; NOAA says it updates the current year's file each month, so the 2026 counts can change. The counts here leave out 97 more rows that NOAA files under forecast zones named Lake, such as High Wind and winter weather entries.

A few dates account for much of the total. July 15, 2024 and March 30, 2025 each carry 12 wind entries, March 19, 2025 carries 11 and March 15, 2025 carries 10.

The narratives name two derechos. For August 10, 2020, NOAA writes that a line of intense thunderstorms known as a derecho "brought widespread severe wind damage across Iowa, northern Illinois and northern Indiana," and the same narrative reports that in northwest Indiana the winds downed a very large number of trees and damaged homes and other buildings. For July 15, 2024 the narrative opens: "A derecho moved across northwest Indiana during the evening of July 15th producing widespread wind damage and three tornadoes, one of which started in northeast Illinois."

Individual entries tie the wind to rooftops. On June 10, 2020 a Dyer entry says "Roof damage was reported to a residential structure near Calumet Avenue and 213th Street." On July 28, 2023 a Munster entry reads "Sections of a roof were blown off of a home on Timrick Drive in Munster." Two entries from Cedar Lake on March 15, 2025 say "Roofing and siding damage occurred around the neighborhood north of Hanover Central High School" and "Clay roofing tiles were ripped off of a church along 133rd Ave in Cedar Lake." Fifteen days later, a March 30, 2025 entry notes that "Shingles were damaged on a house in the Latitude subdivision in Winfield."

Hail is a smaller part of the file than wind. Twenty-five of the 28 hail entries give a diameter of 1.00 inch or more, and every one of the 18 hail days has at least one such entry. The largest figures are 1.75 inches, filed under St. John on July 7, 2017 and Dyer on June 10, 2020; three more entries sit at 1.50 inches. March has the most hail entries by month, 9 on 5 days, and no entry anywhere in the file reaches 2.00 inches.

The file's first tornado entry for the county is dated March 14, 2019, and 12 of the 22 are dated 2025 or 2026. Of the 22, fifteen are rated EF0, five EF1, one EF2 and one EF3. NOAA's narrative for the EF1 of March 31, 2023, which began in Merrillville, says it "blew out windows and tore sections of roofs off of multiple homes." A December 10, 2021 EF0 that ran from Cedar Lake toward Crown Point is described as producing "scattered light to moderate roof damage." Of March 19, 2025, when four tornadoes are filed, one in Gary "ripped off the roofs of several homes on west 21st Avenue." A July 15, 2024 EF1 that crossed in from Will County, Illinois, is described with "Numerous shingles were removed from a house on 121st Avenue" and "a house suffered significant siding damage" on 120th Avenue.

Five tornado entries share June 11, 2026. The EF2 filed under Lottaville moved through central Merrillville with estimated peak winds of 120 mph, and NOAA's narrative closes with the sentence: "At least 200 buildings and houses were damaged with over 60 houses suffering major damage." An EF0 the same day in Cedar Lake caused "roofing and siding damage to multiple houses," and an EF3 with estimated winds of 165 mph moved through rural parts of the county, where the narrative describes tree and crop damage.

NOAA's Indiana state climate summary adds regional context. It says that in northwestern Indiana Lake Michigan moderates temperature and is "the source of lake-effect snows," and recounts that a January 21 to 22, 2014 lake effect storm "dropped 18 inches of snow in Gary over a 5-hour period." Permits and local rules

Lake County's Plan Commission publishes a "Procedure for Requesting a Building Permit" that gives 219-755-3700 for questions. Its licensing language refers to an "Unincorporated Licensing Board." It requires that applications "must be signed by the owner of the property or a contractor holding a CURRENT LAKE COUNTY CONTRACTOR'S LICENSE" and says contractors "shall be licensed per Contractor License Ordinance #1628." The procedure does not mention roofing.

Towns and cities set their own rules. Crown Point's Building Department answers the question in its FAQ: "No permit is needed if you're only re-roofing or re-shingling. A permit is required if you're replacing rafters or sheeting." Merrillville's building permit page lists new construction, additions, pools, sheds, fences, detached garages, decks, signs, and excavation and fill; re-roofing is not on that list, and the page says "Merrillville requires that all contractors be licensed with the Town." Hammond licenses by trade: its contractor license application, revised January 8, 2026, offers "Residential Roofing" and "Exterior Remodeling" as license types, with an open-book exam, a one-hour limit and a 70 percent passing score. None of these pages is a substitute for a call to the office that serves your street.

What to settle before you ask for bids

- Which jurisdiction your address falls in: the county Plan Commission, or the building office of your own town or city.

- Whether your town expects the work to be done by someone it licenses, and whether the written bids mention the permit question at all.

- The date of the most recent storm to reach your street, with photos taken afterward.

- The year your roof was last replaced, if any record exists.
