Roofing issues to look for after a storm

  • Hail bruising and granule loss — impact marks that shorten shingle life even when no leak appears immediately.
  • Flashing and penetration damage — check chimneys, vents, and valleys, where post-storm leaks can start.
  • Impact damage that hides from the ground — hail strikes are hard to see without getting on the roof, which is why documentation matters.
  • Partial-slope damage — storms can damage one or two elevations, raising repair-versus-replace questions that comparing bids can help you think through.
  • Slow leaks after a storm — minor damage can go unnoticed until water stains show indoors.

What to expect locally

The practical sequence for Fairfield County homeowners after storm damage: document everything with photos before any cleanup, review your policy and ask your insurer how to report damage, and gather written repair bids so you can compare scope and pricing side by side. Nothing in that sequence requires committing to a contractor early — you can keep your options open while you review any written bids you receive.

Ask any contractor for an itemized written estimate, proof of insurance, and local references. The process can take longer after a widespread storm, when repair schedules fill up. Patience and paperwork usually serve homeowners better than speed and pressure.

Season and timing

Post-storm weeks can be congested, and quotes gathered in a hurry may be harder to compare; the weeks after the rush can be a calmer time to review bids. Whatever the calendar says, the sequence stays the same: document first, understand your policy second, compare any written bids you receive third — and take your time rather than rushing to agree with the first person who knocks on the door.

Permit paperwork in Fairfield County

No single office answers this countywide. The Fairfield County Building Department, 138 West Chestnut Street, Lancaster (740-652-7130), calls its job plan review, permits and inspections for commercial work across unincorporated Fairfield County, Violet Township excepted, and regulates structures apart from residential structures housing up to three families. Its listing also credits the Regional Planning Commission with performing the department's administrative functions. Planning's own description reads differently: it administers that department for residential along with commercial construction. Because the two listings frame scope differently, homeowners in a township should phone first.

The city of Lancaster keeps a separate office, 104 East Main Street (740-687-6649). Its site says "Most work involving new construction or remodeling requires a building permit. Work classified as maintenance does not." The city's no-permit sheet (revised 07/2024) exempts gutter and downspout work, with no mention of roofing; the minor-permit form has a "RE-ROOFING" checkbox. A city worksheet for roof jobs (revised 10/27/21) asks for layer count, decking, slope, underlayment and ice protection; it also tells roofers they "MUST REGISTER TO WORK" inside Lancaster. Elsewhere the city says a majority of contractors must register, and that an ordinance requires owners to hire a registered one.

Pickerington and Violet Township each keep a building office. In the city proper, the city's department at 100 Lockville Road (614-837-3974) holds state certification to enforce both the residential and the commercial codes, and takes contractor registrations plus a "Residential (Existing)" permit form through its OpenGov portal; that page never names roofing. Violet Township describes the township's Building Department as serving the unincorporated area of its community (10190 Blacklick-Eastern Road, 614-575-5556) and cautions that the projects it lists as needing permits are only examples. It names rafters and trusses but names no roofing item, and its exempt list covers siding replacement plus window or door swaps of the same size. Its list advises contacting the department prior to starting a project, to verify whether a permit is required.

The remaining local governments in the county were not checked. Section 4740.01 of the state code licenses electrical, hydronics, plumbing, refrigeration and HVAC trades; roofing is not on that list.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to be home when my contractor or insurer inspects the roof?

For the exterior portion, usually not — but being present means you see the documented damage yourself and can ask questions of whoever inspects the roof.

Should I repair or replace after partial-slope damage?

Shingle availability and the age of the roof both come into it; your insurer can tell you how your policy addresses matching. Written bids that price both paths let you compare the two side by side.
