The Middle Tennessee climate and your roofing

NWS Nashville's heavy precipitation climatology for Middle Tennessee, based on 1961 to 1990 data from 43 cooperative stations, calls November through May the wet season, when two-thirds of annual precipitation falls. It gives March as the wettest month at 5.76 inches and October as the driest at 3.38 inches. These are regional figures for the area that includes Robertson County.

Roofing issues to look for after a storm

  • Slow leaks after a storm — minor damage can go unnoticed until water stains show indoors.
  • Partial-slope damage — storms can damage one or two elevations, raising repair-versus-replace questions that comparing bids can help you think through.
  • Flashing and penetration damage — check chimneys, vents, and valleys, where post-storm leaks can start.
  • Decking and underlayment issues — discovered only at tear-off, and can be a source of change orders worth understanding in advance.
  • Ventilation and code items — older roofs may need code-related upgrades; ask your contractor and your building department how local code items are handled.

What to expect locally

Homeowners in Robertson County navigating a storm claim juggle three parallel tracks: the insurance process (adjuster inspection, scope, settlement), the contractor process (bids, scheduling, materials), and their own documentation. Keeping those tracks separate is the single most useful habit — your insurer decides coverage under your policy; your contractor proposes the repair scope; and written bids let you compare scope and pricing side by side.

Ask any contractor for an itemized written estimate, proof of insurance, and local references. The process can take longer after a widespread storm, when repair schedules fill up. Patience and paperwork usually serve homeowners better than speed and pressure.

Season and timing

Post-storm weeks can be congested, and quotes gathered in a hurry may be harder to compare; the weeks after the rush can be a calmer time to review bids. Whatever the calendar says, the sequence stays the same: document first, understand your policy second, compare any written bids you receive third — and take your time rather than rushing to agree with the first person who knocks on the door.

Robertson County turns up 172 times as a county-typed hail, thunderstorm wind or tornado row in NOAA's yearly detail files, 2016 through 2026. Hail supplies 40 of them, spread across 20 days between May 10, 2016 and June 9, 2026. 2024 was the heavy year for hail: 12 rows on 3 dates. 2018 logged 7 rows over 5 dates, 2025 logged 7 over 3, and 2016 logged 6 over 2. The quiet years: 2017, 2019 and 2023 produced just 2 rows per year, 2022 and 2026 produced 1 row per year, and 2020 and 2021 produced nothing. Stones measured 0.75 inch on 5 rows and 0.88 inch on 5 more, while 1.00 inch is the commonest reading, at 17 rows. A 1.25 reading occurs once, 1.50 on 4 rows, 1.75 on 5, 2.00 on 2, and a lone row reached 2.50. Thirty rows, on 13 dates, hold stones of 1.00 and up. Hail clusters in spring: April, May or June claims 34 of the 40 rows, spanning 14 dates.

Labeled Cross Plains, Gause, Coopertown and Crunk, four rows hit 1.75 inches in one May 2016 night. The CST stamps are 20:50, 21:06, 21:14 and 21:30; in CDT they read 21:50, 22:06, 22:14 and 22:30. NWS's episode summary calls the wider event "The largest severe weather event of Spring 2016 across Middle Tennessee". The other large hail date, 2024-05-08, supplies seven rows, led by a 2.50 at Youngville; its stamp, 14:15 CST, becomes 15:15 CDT.

Wind: 123 thunderstorm rows on 70 days, opening with 2016-03-31 and closing with 2026-04-16. NOAA gives 119 of them its EG flag (estimated) and four its MG flag (measured). Only eight fall below 50 knots, bottoming out at 43. 2016 stands highest for wind, with 22 rows spanning 12 dates, and 2024 follows with 16 spanning 9. Summer leads: June plus July yield 54 rows. The ceiling is 70 knots, two estimated rows at Springfield on June 21, 2019, both 18:30 CST (19:30 CDT). A measured Springfield row in that minute reached 68 knots (78 mph, per its spotter).

Nine tornado rows fall on five days. NWS survey crews get the credit for each. The single EF2 ran from Springfield to Oakland on December 9, 2023. The 16:16 stamp is CST, and that December date sits in the standard-time half of the year, so it equals the local clock. The narrative includes "The tornado moved into southern Springfield near Fairway Trail and Kemper Court". The EF1 group: Adams to Barren Plain twice on February 24, 2018 (15:53 and 21:19 CST, both standard time; 1.24 and 3.59 miles); on 2024-05-08, Stroudsville to Gause ran 2.2 miles from a 13:43 CST (14:43 CDT) start; Ridgeton to Ridge Top on April 28, 2026 (01:29 CST, 02:29 CDT; 0.92 mile). May 4, 2021 brought four EF0 rows. Their stamps run 04:56, 05:01, 05:02, 05:10 on the CST clock, or 05:56, 06:01, 06:02, 06:10 in CDT. NWS's note on that morning: "This line of thunderstorms spawned a total of 14 EF-0 tornadoes throughout Middle Tennessee".

NOAA also files rows under forecast zones instead of counties, and some carry Robertson's name; none feed these totals. Nine among them are wind rows (seven classed Strong Wind, a pair classed High Wind), and the rest cover other kinds of weather.

Maps and clocks

All 172 rows come with a map point. Census geocoding sorts 168 of the points into Robertson County; the other four land in Davidson County. Those four share the Ridge Top label (one hail, three wind). The totals follow whichever county NOAA wrote on the row, not the geocoder's answer. Per NOAA's guide to the columns, times are "usually in LST", and Robertson's are fixed to CST, so summer stamps sit an hour behind the wall clock. That affects 152 of the 172 rows. One crosses midnight: the Coopertown wind report from June 23, 2016 sits at 23:10 on the CST clock; one hour later, on the daylight clock, it is 00:10 CDT, early June 24. The 2026 file is revised monthly, so its counts may drift.

Springfield and the county both take permit paperwork

Two Springfield offices describe permit duties across Robertson County, and their pages overlap. The city codes department, 405 North Main Street, (615) 382-2200, open 7:30am to 4:30pm (a lunch break is shown from noon to 1pm), writes that it "handles permitting, inspections, property maintenance complaints and plan review for future development throughout Robertson County." Its opening line, though, ties the department's safety duties to structures inside Springfield itself. The page titled "Do I need a Permit?" lists sample jobs that need a permit; they include replacing a roof's covering, whether shingles or another type, and the page calls the sampling partial. The inspection page says an inspector will visit inside three working days of a request.

Planning at the county, 527 South Brown Street, (615) 384-3666, taking visitors weekdays between 8:00 am and 4:30 pm, counts "Issues and maintains records of all building permits" among its duties. Its walkthrough says permits are "issued to the Landowner or Contractor only" and expects each contractor to hold a current license and be entered in the system; the online form says requests get reviewed inside two to three working days. The walkthrough covers septic, water and driveway paperwork without once mentioning re-roofing. A 1979 private act for Robertson County, hosted at the University of Tennessee by its advisory service for counties, makes a county permit depend on an alteration being worth more than $3,000, and exempts cities that already require permits of their own, as well as certain planning regions; the office can say whether that act still reaches a re-roof.

Greenbrier runs its own Building Department, 2414 Hwy 41, (615) 643-4531. Its application requests the contractor's state license number in a labeled section; the checklist of improvements has no roofing entry. White House, Cross Plains, Coopertown, Cedar Hill and Ridge Top all appear as storm labels, yet none of their offices was read.

Two state license classes

The licensing board that regulates contractors in Tennessee wants a license held by prime contractors on projects of $25,000 or more; roofing subcontractors working under another contractor need it when their own portion reaches that figure. Under that sits a Home Improvement class for residential remodeling work valued from $3,000 through $24,999, a tier the state extends only to counties that chose to adopt it. That page names nine, with Robertson among them; roofing appears in its home-improvement list.

Loose ends before you compare bids

- Pick the counter that serves your address: Springfield's codes office, county Planning, or Greenbrier.

- Call it and ask about a roof permit for your house, including who files.

- Learn whether the permit fee sits inside each bid or is billed separately.

- For each bid, note the board license number cited, and where the price falls against the $3,000 and $25,000 thresholds.

- Your insurer's written estimate, if one exists, shown to every bidder.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to be home when my contractor or insurer inspects the roof?

For the exterior portion, usually not — but being present means you see the documented damage yourself and can ask questions of whoever inspects the roof.

Who decides whether my policy applies to roof damage in Robertson County?

Your insurer decides coverage under the terms of your policy. Read your policy, ask your adjuster what is included, and document the damage with dated photos. Our guide on filing a property damage claim walks through the process step by step.
