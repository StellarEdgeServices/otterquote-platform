A severe storm in St. Charles County can leave roofs with the full menu of problems: hail bruising, wind-lifted shingles, damaged flashing, and the slow leaks that follow. Getting more than one written bid is one way to understand what a repair may cost.

The Northeast Prairie climate and your roofing

The Climate Center lists "high-intensity rains, protracted drought, heat waves and cold waves, ice storms, windstorms, and tornadoes" among Missouri's extreme climatic events and says that "All of Missouri experiences 'extreme' climate events, and such events must be considered part of the normal climate." NOAA's summary adds that "Missouri has experienced an increase in the number of extreme precipitation events," and that for large portions of the state "more than 40% of the total annual precipitation occurs on the 10 wettest days of the year." These are statewide or regional figures, not measurements taken in St. Charles County.

Roofing issues to look for after a storm

  • Decking and underlayment issues — discovered only at tear-off, and can be a source of change orders worth understanding in advance.
  • Impact damage that hides from the ground — hail strikes are hard to see without getting on the roof, which is why documentation matters.
  • Partial-slope damage — storms can damage one or two elevations, raising repair-versus-replace questions that comparing bids can help you think through.
  • Wind-lifted and creased shingles — broken seal strips let later storms drive rain under the roof surface.
  • Slow leaks after a storm — minor damage can go unnoticed until water stains show indoors.

What to expect locally

The practical sequence for St. Charles County homeowners after storm damage: document everything with photos before any cleanup, review your policy and ask your insurer how to report damage, and gather written repair bids so you can compare scope and pricing side by side. Nothing in that sequence requires committing to a contractor early — you can keep your options open while you review any written bids you receive.

Be appropriately skeptical of anyone who shows up unsolicited after a storm, pressures you to sign paperwork on the spot, or quotes a price without getting on the roof or examining the damage up close. Storm-chasing crews can show up after any major storm, and one practical defense is unhurried, written bids that you can check against each other.

Season and timing

Post-storm weeks can be congested, and quotes gathered in a hurry may be harder to compare; the weeks after the rush can be a calmer time to review bids. Whatever the calendar says, the sequence stays the same: document first, understand your policy second, compare any written bids you receive third — and take your time rather than rushing to agree with the first person who knocks on the door.

The Census Bureau's 2020 to 2024 American Community Survey (5-year table B25034) counts 167,163 housing units in St. Charles County and puts the median year built at 1994. The 2000s are the largest band with 35,465 units (21.2 percent), then the 1990s with 32,538, the 1980s with 28,267, the 2010s with 24,017 and the 1970s with 19,726. Only 41,383 units, or 24.8 percent, were built before 1980, and 5,493 date from 2020 or later. Most of the county's houses are therefore from a period when asphalt shingles were the usual roof surface, but the survey does not say what is on any one roof, so check yours.

St. Charles County's Community Development pages describe two permits that can matter for exterior work. Its Land Use Permit page says the county requires land use permits "to construct, alter, or repair of any building or structure," and that "Applications for building permits also serve as a land use permit application." Its Residential Building Permit page lists barns, fences over six feet, detached garages, decks, sheds and swimming pools, among other projects, and roofing is not named in that list; it gives the Building and Code Enforcement Division at 636-949-7345. Because the county's pages do not settle whether a re-roof needs one, and because many addresses are inside cities that run their own offices, call before work begins.

The county's 2021 residential code amendments also speak to shingle roofs. Section R905.2.8.5 says "A drip edge shall be provided at eaves and rake edges of shingle roofs," with segments overlapped "not less than 2 inches." Section R905.2.8.2 says "Valley linings shall be installed in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions before applying shingles." Those are provisions of an adopted code, not a statement about any particular roof.

A great many homes in St. Charles County sit in subdivisions where the roof is a big visible part of the street, so questions of color and style matter more than they might elsewhere. Ask whether your homeowners association or subdivision indenture sets rules about shingle type or color, and get that answer in writing before you order anything. A bid that names the exact shingle line and color lets you check it against any such rules.

Next consider the shape of the roof. Many houses have several gables, dormers, a garage roof at a different pitch and a porch. Ask whoever prepares each bid to write down how many separate roof areas were counted, and whether a steep or hard-to-reach area is treated differently. Ask about the walking surface, the ladders or lifts, and what is being done to protect the plantings and the driveway.

Ask about attic ventilation and insulation, because a new roof is a good time to look at both. Find out whether ridge vent, box vents or fans are being installed, and whether the bid states anything about baffles at the eaves. Ask if the old decking is inspected for damp or damaged boards, and how replacement of those boards will be documented and priced.

Keep the manufacturer's literature, a color sample, and the written warranty. Take pictures of the existing roof and the gutters first.

It also helps to ask how the finished job will be checked. A walk around the property at the end, with the written bid in hand, lets you tick off the items one by one: ridge cap, pipe boots, flashing, drip edge, cleanup of the lawn and flower beds. Ask in advance whether a final photograph set is part of the paperwork, and ask when the leftover shingles will be hauled away instead of stacked beside the garage.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to be home when my contractor or insurer inspects the roof?

For the exterior portion, usually not — but being present means you see the documented damage yourself and can ask questions of whoever inspects the roof.

Who decides whether my policy applies to roof damage in St. Charles County?

Your insurer decides coverage under the terms of your policy. Read your policy, ask your adjuster what is included, and document the damage with dated photos. Our guide on filing a property damage claim walks through the process step by step.
