A gutter system has two jobs: move rain away from the foundation, and carry whatever load winter puts on it. When hail flattens the profile or pulls fasteners loose, both jobs suffer, and the resulting water problems show up at the foundation and fascia long before the gutters themselves look obviously broken.

The Northeast Prairie climate and your gutters

NOAA's state summary says Missouri's "location in the interior of North America and the lack of mountain barriers to the north and south expose the state to incursions of cold arctic air masses in the winter and warm, moist air masses from the Gulf of Mexico in the summer." The Climate Center says the northern half of the state averages about 110 winter days with temperatures below freezing. It says snow has been known to fall as early as October and as late as May, and that "most of it falls in December, January, and February." These are statewide or regional figures, not measurements taken in St. Charles County.

Gutter issues to look for after a storm

  • Fascia and soffit rot — the downstream cost of gutter systems that stopped doing their job quietly.
  • Hail-flattened profiles — dents that reduce water-carrying capacity and often point to damage on the roof above.
  • Pulled fasteners and sagging runs — ice and debris load that separates gutters from fascia over a winter.
  • Downspout crushing and disconnects — drainage failures that surface as foundation and grading problems.
  • Seam and end-cap leaks — often storm-initiated, always worse after a freeze cycle.

What to expect locally

The practical sequence for St. Charles County homeowners after storm damage: document everything with photos before any cleanup, review your policy and ask your insurer how to report damage, and gather written repair bids so you can compare scope and pricing side by side. Nothing in that sequence requires committing to a contractor early — you can keep your options open while you review any written bids you receive.

Ask any contractor for an itemized written estimate, proof of insurance, and local references. The process can take longer after a widespread storm, when repair schedules fill up. Patience and paperwork usually serve homeowners better than speed and pressure.

Season and timing

Timing is worth planning. The weeks after a storm can be busy for adjusters and contractors, and winter weather can limit exterior work. Document the damage first, then take the time you need to compare any written bids you receive.

St. Charles County notes

Water leaves a clear mark in the county's NOAA record. For January 2016 through December 2025, the Storm Events Database holds 8 flash flood entries and 3 flood entries for St. Charles County. The flash floods fall in April, May, June (2), July (2) and August (2), one or two a month, and none in the other months. The July 26, 2022 entry filed under Foristell is the heaviest: "Up to 13 inches of rain fell in a 6 to 10 hour period causing widespread flash flooding," with Interstate 70 flooded "with up to five feet of water around the intersection with Mid Rivers Mall Drive." The same narrative says "One hundred seventeen buildings were damaged from the flooding" and that 87 people were evacuated from homes or businesses.

The August 8, 2020 entry filed under Orchard Farm says up to 7 inches fell in about four hours, that "Several basements were flooded and at least one home near Belleau Creek had several feet of water in it," and that residents of 12 units were evacuated from an apartment building on Riverview Lane. The July 10, 2019 entry filed under Fort Zumwalt says up to three inches fell in about an hour and a half and that Belleau Creek "flooded Belleau Creek Road with over 6 inches of water." The August 26, 2019 entry filed under Augusta says portions of Femme Osage Creek Road "washed out" after up to five inches fell in the southwest part of the county. On June 4, 2025 an entry filed under Harvester says neighborhood yards "also had about 1 foot of water."

River flooding is filed separately and mostly on the Mississippi side. The May 6, 2019 entry filed under Elm Pt, which NOAA files as a Flash Flood and not as a Flood, says "The Elm Point Levee had a major breach allowing the flood waters from the Mississippi River to rapidly flow in." The June 2, 2019 Flood entry filed under West Alton says "A number of homes and businesses were flooded in West Alton, Portage Des Sioux, Orchard Farm and Kampville areas," with about 5 buildings destroyed, about 500 with major damage and around 1300 with minor damage. A January 1, 2016 entry filed under Augusta says the Missouri River remained at moderate flood stage "as a result of record rainfall in late December."

Wind-and-tree entries mention gutters too. The July 15, 2020 entry filed under Gilmore says a limb fell onto a house in St. Peters "causing minor roof and gutter damage." The August 12, 2023 entry filed under Wentzville Airport says a branch fell on the north side of St. Charles "causing minor roof, soffit and gutter damage."

St. Charles County also publishes floodplain rules that matter after water damage. The Floodplain Development Permit entry on the county's Permits page says the county requires floodplain development permits "for practically all construction, improvements, demolition, or earthmoving" in areas the 2016 flood maps define as floodway fringe, density floodway or floodway, and that they "are required even in cases when a building permit would not." Its Substantial Damage and Improvement page describes a "50% rule" for structures in those areas, defining substantial damage as when the cost to repair a structure to its previous condition "would equal or exceed 50% of the market value of the structure." That page gives the Planning and Zoning Division at 636-949-7335 for help in understanding how the county applies the rule. These pages apply to unincorporated parts of the county and to mapped flood areas only, and a gutter replacement is a very different matter from a rebuild.

On a long gutter run under a big roofline, the details that matter are the ones that deal with a lot of water at once. Start by looking at the roof valleys and the places where a high roof empties onto a lower one; those spots can overshoot a standard gutter. Mention each such spot to everyone who prepares a bid, and ask what the bid proposes for it.

Ask for the size of the gutter, the size of the downspouts, and how many downspouts are planned for each side of the house. Compare the number from bid to bid. Ask what material is used, the color, and whether the run is made on site in a single length. Ask what the hangers are and how far apart they will go, along with what is behind them to hold the load.

Ask where each downspout leads. If it ends at a driveway, a patio or a neighbor's yard, ask what the bid offers as a fix. Extensions, buried drain pipe and splash pads each have a place, and the bid should name what is being installed instead of saying drainage will be handled.

Ask whether any guard system is being quoted as a separate item, and what its warranty says. Photograph the current gutters, fascia and downspout outlets before work. Keep a copy of every page you sign.

Think, too, about the order of work when the roof and the gutters are both on your list. Fascia boards get exposed when old shingles come off, so some owners prefer to have the gutters hung after the new roof edge is in. Ask whoever prepares each bid how that sequence is meant to work and whether a second visit is part of the price or an extra line.

Frequently asked questions

Should I document dented gutters?

Yes. Hail that dents gutters has often hit the roof too, so gutter dents are worth photographing both as a repair item and as a sign of the storm's severity. Ask your adjuster whether they are included in the scope.

What size hail dents aluminum gutters?

Smaller than most people expect — gutters often show impact evidence from hail that left shingles looking intact from the ground, which is why they're a standard inspection point.
