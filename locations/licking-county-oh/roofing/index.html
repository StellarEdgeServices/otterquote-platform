A severe storm in Licking County can leave roofs with the full menu of problems: hail bruising, wind-lifted shingles, damaged flashing, and the slow leaks that follow. Getting more than one written bid is one way to understand what a repair may cost.

The central Ohio climate and your roofing

NWS Wilmington's summary of April 17, 2024 confirms an EF1 tornado in Delaware County, which is in the Central division with Licking County. NOAA's Ohio state climate summary says dangerous storms can occur in every season, and it lists snowstorms and ice storms among winter weather. It lists thunderstorms, flood-producing rainstorms, hail and tornadoes among the convective storms it calls common in the warmer months.

Roofing issues to look for after a storm

  • Ventilation and code items — older roofs may need code-related upgrades; ask your contractor and your building department how local code items are handled.
  • Wind-lifted and creased shingles — broken seal strips let later storms drive rain under the roof surface.
  • Slow leaks after a storm — minor damage can go unnoticed until water stains show indoors.
  • Hail bruising and granule loss — impact marks that shorten shingle life even when no leak appears immediately.
  • Decking and underlayment issues — discovered only at tear-off, and can be a source of change orders worth understanding in advance.

What to expect locally

The practical sequence for Licking County homeowners after storm damage: document everything with photos before any cleanup, review your policy and ask your insurer how to report damage, and gather written repair bids so you can compare scope and pricing side by side. Nothing in that sequence requires committing to a contractor early — you can keep your options open while you review any written bids you receive.

Local demand can also move in waves. After a widely publicized hail event, contractors across a region may get busy at once. If you receive more than one written bid, comparing them gives you a way to check pricing when demand spikes, and can surface scope differences — what one bidder saw that another missed — before the work starts rather than after.

Season and timing

Timing is worth planning. The weeks after a storm can be busy for adjusters and contractors, and winter weather can limit exterior work. Document the damage first, then take the time you need to compare any written bids you receive.

What the Census counts

The American Community Survey's table DP04 (Census Bureau, five-year 2020-2024 estimates) puts Licking County at 73,273 housing units: 68,620 occupied, 4,653 vacant (6.4 percent). Homeowners occupy 50,603 of the occupied units (73.7 percent) and tenants 18,017 (26.3 percent). Detached houses number 55,654 (76.0 percent of all units) and mobile homes 3,502 (4.8 percent).

The build years split almost evenly around 1980. Units dated before 1980 total 36,750 (50.2 percent); 36,523 were built in 1980 or later. The 2000s form the largest decade, 11,973 units (16.3 percent), ahead of the 11,290 built in 1939 or earlier (15.4 percent) and the 1990s with 11,137 (15.2 percent). Since 2000 the tally is 18,076 (24.7 percent). Table B25035 gives a median year built of 1980, with a margin of error of 1. A building's age is not its roof's age, since roofs get replaced.

Who handles a roof permit around Newark

The Licking County Building Code Department, 675 Price Road, Newark, (740) 349-6671, keeps hours of 7:00am to 4:00pm, Monday through Friday. Its page calls the department "responsible for the enforcement of building codes for all residential and commercial projects throughout the majority of Licking County" and says "If you are not sure if a permit is required, please contact our office." The department's guidance letter on roof asphalt shingle replacement, revised May 15, 2026, says "Licking County considers roof asphalt shingle replacement to be a minor repair, and as such, does not require an approval or inspection through this office." The work must still meet the Residential Code of Ohio, and the letter says replacing at least half of the sheathing, at least half of the rafters, or one or more pre-engineered trusses "does require approval and inspection through our office." It speaks only of asphalt shingles. The department's "Summary of residential work that does or does not require approval", dated 2015, lists "Shingle replacement" under "A PERMIT IS NOT REQUIRED FOR". The county page also says "you must first obtain a Zoning Certificate from your local Zoning Inspector."

Newark's Engineering and Zoning office, City Hall, 40 West Main Street, zoning line (740) 670-7729, states that the county department "is the agency responsible for the enforcement of residential and commercial building codes within the City of Newark." Its zoning-permit link is labeled "new dwelling, room additions, porch w/roof, change of occupancy"; re-roofing goes unnamed in that list, which settles nothing either way.

Granville's page is the one I read that names re-roofing. Its Planning and Development page, (740) 587-3997, says "The only types of construction that are exempt from a zoning permit are exact replacements (such as replacing steps) or the re-roofing of a building or structure", then adds that a replacement which is not exact and "alters the building or structure architecturally" needs a zoning permit. Building permits are issued on the village's behalf by the county department, and property inside Granville's Architectural Review Overlay District must meet added architectural standards.

Heath's Building and Zoning Department, 1287 Hebron Road, (740) 522-1420, says "If you plan any activity that is not exempted, you are required to apply for a zoning permit at the Municipal Building at 1287 Hebron Rd." Neither its 2026 fee schedule nor its residential zoning application mentions roofing. Pataskala's Planning and Zoning Office answers at (740) 927-4910; the part of its permits page I could read shows no roofing rule. We did not read Johnstown, Hebron, Utica, Buckeye Lake, or any township office; ask the one that serves your address.

Before the bids go out

- The insurer's written estimate, if one exists, handed unchanged to every bidder. - The zoning office for your address: Newark, Heath, Granville, Pataskala, or your township's zoning inspector. - A question for the county building department if the job goes beyond asphalt shingles or touches the trusses, rafters or sheathing: is approval needed, and who files it? - If the house is in Granville, a call about the overlay district before materials are picked.

Frequently asked questions

Should I repair or replace after partial-slope damage?

Shingle availability and the age of the roof both come into it; your insurer can tell you how your policy addresses matching. Written bids that price both paths let you compare the two side by side.

Do I need to be home when my contractor or insurer inspects the roof?

For the exterior portion, usually not — but being present means you see the documented damage yourself and can ask questions of whoever inspects the roof.
