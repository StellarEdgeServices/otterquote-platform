It also lists snowstorms and ice storms as winter weather in Ohio.

Roofing issues to look for after a storm

  • Flashing and penetration damage — check chimneys, vents, and valleys, where post-storm leaks can start.
  • Slow leaks after a storm — minor damage can go unnoticed until water stains show indoors.
  • Hail bruising and granule loss — impact marks that shorten shingle life even when no leak appears immediately.
  • Impact damage that hides from the ground — hail strikes are hard to see without getting on the roof, which is why documentation matters.
  • Partial-slope damage — storms can damage one or two elevations, raising repair-versus-replace questions that comparing bids can help you think through.

What to expect locally

Homeowners in Warren County navigating a storm claim juggle three parallel tracks: the insurance process (adjuster inspection, scope, settlement), the contractor process (bids, scheduling, materials), and their own documentation. Keeping those tracks separate is the single most useful habit — your insurer decides coverage under your policy; your contractor proposes the repair scope; and written bids let you compare scope and pricing side by side.

Local demand can also move in waves. After a widely publicized hail event, contractors across a region may get busy at once. If you receive more than one written bid, comparing them gives you a way to check pricing when demand spikes, and can surface scope differences — what one bidder saw that another missed — before the work starts rather than after.

Season and timing

Timing matters in Warren County. The weeks after a storm can be busy for adjusters and contractors. Documentation is the part that does not depend on the calendar: photograph damage once it is safe and note the date for your adjuster. Ask your insurer whether your policy sets a deadline for reporting damage.

Storm record for Warren County

Hail comes first. For Warren County, NOAA's Storm Events Database lists 28 hail rows, falling on 16 separate dates between April 26, 2016 and June 28, 2025. No year from 2016 through 2025 lacks a hail row, and the 2026 file we read holds none. We repeat NOAA's own row dates without shifting them: its bulk-file format guide labels the clock field "usually in LST", while every Warren County row we counted carries the EST stamp. Eighteen of the 28 hail rows, spread across 9 dates, record a stone of 1.00 inch or larger.

The largest stone and the busiest hail day coincide on April 8, 2020. Seven rows share it: 2.00 inches under Socialville, 1.50 under Mason, 1.00 inch under Loveland Park and twice more under Mason, plus two 0.88-inch rows labeled Mason and Fosters. Four rows from April 26, 2016 come from Roachester (1.75), Middleboro (1.25), Windsor (1.00) and Blackhawk (0.88), and three from July 18, 2023 read 1.50 under Middleboro, 1.00 under Middleboro and 1.00 under Mason. April is the biggest hail month, with 12 rows across 3 dates; May contributes 5 rows, one per date, July 4 rows across 2 dates and February 3 rows across 2 dates. Place names in these rows are NOAA's reference labels, so read these as pointers to a general area and not as street addresses.

Wind is the longer ledger: 140 thunderstorm wind rows on 63 different days between May 10, 2016 and April 28, 2026. Instruments produced the speed in just 3 rows; the strongest, a 59-knot reading labeled Lebanon Warren Co Ar on May 27, 2019, carries a narrative placing the measurement at the airport. Estimates account for the remaining 137. June leads the calendar with 51 rows over 20 days, followed by July with 25 over 14, April with 19 over 6 and May with 15 over 9. Year by year, 2025 stands highest with 24 rows on 10 days, and 2017 is the lightest full year with 4 rows on 3 days, while the 2026 file so far holds 2 rows on 1 date. No single day tops March 30, 2025 at 9 rows; June 23, 2016 has 8 and June 13, 2022 has 7.

June 23, 2016 also holds the highest estimate, 70 knots, in three rows labeled Springboro, Edgetown and Crosswick. NOAA's narrative for the Springboro row names Settlers Walk Boulevard, Stolle Drive and Remick Boulevard inside a swath of damage that reached southeast toward Five Points and Lytle, and it puts the estimate at up to 80 mph. That date also produced the longest tornado path in the county's file.

Zone-filed winds are listed by forecast zone and not by county. NWS Directive 10-1605 describes High Wind and Strong Wind as "non-convective winds", and the WARREN zone carries 7 High Wind rows on 7 dates between April 3, 2016 and September 27, 2024, six estimated at 50 knots and the last at 52. NOAA's episode text for that last row opens "Remnants of Hurricane Helene moved north through the eastern Tennessee Valley into the southern Ohio Valley." and the event narrative adds "A NWS employee estimated 65 mph winds near Clarksville." The zone also carries 3 Strong Wind rows, dated October 28, 2018, March 13, 2026 and March 15, 2026, all three measured gusts, at 40, 45 and 44 knots in that order.

Thirteen tornado rows cover 8 dates, nine rated EF0 and four EF1. The June 23, 2016 EF1, labeled Edgetown to Middleboro, runs 20.7 miles and up to 250 yards wide in NOAA's fields; its narrative has it first touching down at a campground on State Route 73 near Waynesville. On May 7, 2024 NOAA lists five separate rows: EF0 at Morrow, EF1 from South Lebanon to Stubbs Mills, EF0 from Lebanon to a NOAA reference point labeled Lelan, EF1 from Roachester to Hammel and EF0 at Middleboro. The South Lebanon row covers 2.64 miles at 150 yards wide, and the last sentence of its narrative is "The tornado appeared to end near Stubbs Mill Road." A sixth 2024 row, an EF1 from Hammel to Middleboro, follows on July 30. March 30, 2025 carries two EF0 rows, one labeled Mason to Stubbs Mills at 9.73 miles, whose narrative says it began in Butler County and crossed into Warren County along Butler Warren Road before ending "just west of the Little Miami Junior High School." NOAA adds two more EF0 rows, stamped 2358 EST on April 2 and 0002 EST on April 3, 2025, which converts to 12:58 a.m. daylight time on April 3 for the first row and 1:02 a.m. for the second. Detached houses make up 73,582 units (76.6 percent); attached houses add 6,438, two-unit buildings 1,795, buildings of 20 or more units 2,765 and mobile homes 943. These are counts of housing units and households, and they say nothing about any single roof, so the install date that matters is the one for your own house.

Permits and local rules

Two offices come up first here, and they word their answers differently. The Warren County Building and Zoning Department sits at 406 Justice Drive in Lebanon (Building Inspection, Room 167, telephone 513-695-1290), with its front office listed as open 8:00AM to 5:00PM Monday through Friday except holidays. Its general FAQ puts the question "Do I need a new roof permit?" and answers "Only if you are putting in new trusses/rafters?" [sic]. Asked "Do I have to be registered as a general contractor?", the same FAQ answers "No." It also asks whether the county requires an ice and water shield, answers yes and links a table.

The FAQ speaks to timing as well. Under "Why does it take so long to get a permit?" it answers "The County is growing and due to the large volume of permits, the plans are reviewed in the order that they come in." It says the inspector is in the office from 7:00 to 8:00 each weekday morning and the plans examiner can be seen "8:00 a.m. to 2:00 p.m., Monday, Wednesday, Thursday and Friday", with an appointment made first. A separate list on the page, headed "Provided services to other Counties/Cities", names Clinton County, the City of Wilmington and the City of Franklin, and the page says the department "reviews plans and performs inspections for the above jurisdictions ONLY." Permits for those places are applied for through them.

Mason keeps its own office. The City of Mason Engineering and Building Department, 6000 Mason-Montgomery Road, 513-229-8520, publishes a chart headed "WHY GET A BUILDING PERMIT?" and dated June 2025. Its row "Roof Shingles (replacement)" is marked in the column headed "No Permit Needed", while "Trusses / Replace Plywood on Roof" is marked for a building permit. The chart adds "This list shows only the most common examples of home improvements and is not all inclusive." The city's fee sheet dated 01/01/2026 lists a "ROOF REPLACEMENT (Residential)" permit marked "By Owner’s Request", so ask Mason's office whether your job needs one.

The city's department page asks owners to "reach out to our office early in your planning phases" and sends applicants to the Mason Permit and Application Portal, where the forms include a solar permit application and a residential building and/or electric permit application.

On state licensing, the first definition in Ohio Revised Code section 4740.01 describes a "License" as one issued to an individual as a "heating, ventilating, and air conditioning contractor, refrigeration contractor, electrical contractor, plumbing contractor, or hydronics contractor." Roofing is not one of those five.

What to line up before you ask for bids

- Your insurer's written estimate, if one exists, in a form every bidder you invite can open.

- The office that takes permits for your address: the Warren County building department at Room 167, the City of Mason, the City of Franklin, whose permits the county FAQ says are applied for through the city, or another city's or village's building office.

- A question for each bidder on whether the job touches trusses, rafters or roof plywood, since the county FAQ ties a roof permit to new trusses or rafters and Mason's chart marks trusses and roof plywood for a building permit.

- A time to see the county plans examiner, if your job needs one: the FAQ lists 8:00 a.m. to 2:00 p.m. on Monday, Wednesday, Thursday and Friday, with an appointment made first.
