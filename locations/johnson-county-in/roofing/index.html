The central Indiana climate and your roofing

Johnson County sits in central Indiana. NOAA's 2022 Indiana State Climate Summary says dangerous storms can occur in every season, that winter can bring snow and ice storms, and that thunderstorms capable of producing floods, hail and tornadoes are common in the warmer months. It records that heavy snowstorms during the first week of January 2014 dropped 1 to more than 2 feet of snow over central and northern Indiana.

Roofing issues to look for after a storm

  • Wind-lifted and creased shingles — broken seal strips let later storms drive rain under the roof surface.
  • Partial-slope damage — storms can damage one or two elevations, raising repair-versus-replace questions that comparing bids can help you think through.
  • Impact damage that hides from the ground — hail strikes are hard to see without getting on the roof, which is why documentation matters.
  • Hail bruising and granule loss — impact marks that shorten shingle life even when no leak appears immediately.
  • Decking and underlayment issues — discovered only at tear-off, and can be a source of change orders worth understanding in advance.

What to expect locally

After a storm in Johnson County, the useful work is slow: getting damage documented, raising questions with your insurer, and having the repair scoped carefully. Many homeowners find it helps to slow the process down at the start — documenting damage before tarps and repairs change the evidence, reading their policy before the first phone call, and getting more than one written bid before signing anything.

Ask any contractor for an itemized written estimate, proof of insurance, and local references. The process can take longer after a widespread storm, when repair schedules fill up. Patience and paperwork usually serve homeowners better than speed and pressure.

Season and timing

Storm damage in central Indiana can arrive in clusters: a single hail event can affect many roofs, gutters, and siding elevations in an area at once. Comparing more than one written bid for the same scope is one way homeowners check pricing when demand spikes.

Five permit offices, five different pages

Johnson County's Building Department, 86 W Court St, Franklin, (317) 346-4350, enforces the building code in the unincorporated parts of the county and puts its average review time on a building permit at 10 business days. Its page says the county "does not have any adopted amendments or individual building codes." Roofing is not named on the permit-packet list, which does include a window replacement packet, and the page states that window replacements need permits. The packets themselves were not opened for this research. The county's Contractor Listing page describes a program for unincorporated land that requires a surety bond payable to the county and a listing fee for a one-, two- or five-year term, and it warns that "other municipalities have their own requirements." Confirm the current bond and fee amounts with the office.

Greenwood is the one place where an official page read for this research speaks to roofs directly. Its Building Commissioner, 300 S. Madison Ave., (317) 881-8698, says "If your roof project involves the removal of 25% or more of the decking, a permit is required." The same paragraph continues, "if you are only planning to lay new shingles and patch any areas of decking that are unsound, you will not need to apply for a building permit." The page also links the state sheet on ice and water shield and drip edge. Greenwood's Licensing page lists city licenses from amusements to taxicabs and names no roofing registration.

Franklin's Planning and Economic Development office, 70 E Monroe St, (317) 736-3631, open 8:00 am to 4:00 pm, sends applicants to an online permitting portal, wants inspections scheduled at least one business day ahead, and says permits "MUST be posted on all job sites." Neither roofing nor contractor registration appears on that page. Whiteland's residential permit application, revised 3.2025, says the town "requires a building permit for any building/land improvements that exceed $500" and names the Planning Department, (317) 535-5531, at Town Hall, 549 E. Main St.; roofing is not listed, and the $500 rule is a general one rather than a roofing rule. Bargersville's Town Hall, 24 North Main Street, (317) 422-5115, hosts a Residential Building Permit Application online; nothing read there addresses re-roofing, and no staff or hours were stated, so none are given here.

Where state licensing leaves off

The Indiana Professional Licensing Agency's profession list names plumbing, real estate and home inspectors among others and has no roofing entry. The Attorney General's storm-chaser leaflet tells readers "Licensing requirements vary from community to community" and to check with the city or county building department. For Johnson County, that means the county listing program on unincorporated land and whatever each municipality sets.

Johnson County bid checklist

- Work out which office handles the property: the county, Greenwood, Franklin, Whiteland, Bargersville or another town.

- Ask each bidder whether a re-roof needs a permit there, and who pulls it. Greenwood ties its answer to replacing 25 percent or more of the decking.

- Ask for the bidder's listing or registration status with that same office.

- Ask whether the shingle maker's instructions call for ice and water shield, since the state table says "No" for the county.

- Show every bidder your insurer's written estimate, if one exists.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to be home when my contractor or insurer inspects the roof?

For the exterior portion, usually not — but being present means you see the documented damage yourself and can ask questions of whoever inspects the roof.

Should I repair or replace after partial-slope damage?

Shingle availability and the age of the roof both come into it; your insurer can tell you how your policy addresses matching. Written bids that price both paths let you compare the two side by side.
