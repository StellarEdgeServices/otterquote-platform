A severe storm in Blount County can leave roofs with the full menu of problems: hail bruising, wind-lifted shingles, damaged flashing, and the slow leaks that follow. Getting more than one written bid is one way to understand what a repair may cost.

The East Tennessee climate and your roofing

NWS Morristown's snowfall records show how elevation matters in East Tennessee. For March 12 to 14, 1993, the office lists widespread 24 to 36 inch amounts in the mountains and 40 to 60 inches on the highest peaks of Sevier County. For February 12 to 14, 1960, it lists 10 to 15 inches in the mountains and 5 to 10 inches across the southern valley, an example from the region that includes Blount County.

Roofing issues to look for after a storm

  • Partial-slope damage — storms can damage one or two elevations, raising repair-versus-replace questions that comparing bids can help you think through.
  • Hail bruising and granule loss — impact marks that shorten shingle life even when no leak appears immediately.
  • Slow leaks after a storm — minor damage can go unnoticed until water stains show indoors.
  • Ventilation and code items — older roofs may need code-related upgrades; ask your contractor and your building department how local code items are handled.
  • Impact damage that hides from the ground — hail strikes are hard to see without getting on the roof, which is why documentation matters.

What to expect locally

The practical sequence for Blount County homeowners after storm damage: document everything with photos before any cleanup, review your policy and ask your insurer how to report damage, and gather written repair bids so you can compare scope and pricing side by side. Nothing in that sequence requires committing to a contractor early — you can keep your options open while you review any written bids you receive.

Local demand can also move in waves. After a widely publicized hail event, contractors across a region may get busy at once. If you receive more than one written bid, comparing them gives you a way to check pricing when demand spikes, and can surface scope differences — what one bidder saw that another missed — before the work starts rather than after.

Season and timing

Timing is worth planning. The weeks after a storm can be busy for adjusters and contractors, and winter weather can limit exterior work. Document the damage first, then take the time you need to compare any written bids you receive.

Who handles the permit

Two sets of offices serve the county. Development Services, 303 McGhee Street, Maryville (865-681-9301), describes its job as taking in permit applications and fees, and says it "schedules inspections, maintains permit record files and issues occupancy certificates." The department's manual repeats a rule from the building code: a person who means to alter or repair a structure has to apply to the building official first and get the permit that is required. That manual dates its adopted-codes list to April 17, 2014, yet the department's page says it enforces "the 2018 edition of the International Code Council", so learn which edition governs a replacement roof. Neither county page mentions reroofing, so take the question about a replacement to Development Services.

Inside Maryville city limits, Codes Enforcement works from the lower level of the Municipal Building at 416 W. Broadway Avenue (865-273-3500). Office hours run 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. on weekdays, lunch hour excepted, while inspectors keep narrower windows, 8 to 9 a.m. and 1 to 2 p.m. The Codes page says compliance "is achieved through pre-submittal review meetings with designers, plan review, permit issuance, inspections, and enforcement coordination with other city departments." The city's pamphlet, revised October 2018, says of applications "For single family dwellings the contractor or owner may apply for permits." On booking an inspection it directs readers to the Codes staff and the right inspector. Its FAQ also states "Ordinary minor repairs may be made with the approval of the Building Official without a permit, provided that such repairs do not violate any of the provisions of the building code", citing "replacement of roof shingles" as an example and adding "Call the Codes office to see if permits are required." Whether a full tear-off is a minor repair is for that office to say. Alcoa and the county's other towns were not opened for this page.

On licensing, the county manual says that a job above $25,000 calls for a state contractor's license for building. The Tennessee Board for Licensing Contractors' contractor page sets its own $25,000 line, phrased "or more", and applies it to trade subcontractors too, naming "roofing work when that portion of the project is $25,000 or more, including materials, equipment, and labor." The Board's Home Improvement page applies a lower tier only where a county has opted in, and introduces its nine with the words "Currently, this applies to the following counties"; Blount does not appear among them. The Verify a License link on the Board's pages opens a search titled "Verify Public Search"; that search needs scripts to run, so no lookup was made for this page.

Lining up your Blount County bids

- Pin down the office that is actually yours: Maryville Codes Enforcement, Alcoa, some other town, or Development Services on McGhee Street for addresses outside every town.

- For a roof inside Maryville, choose a call time that suits the inspectors' two short windows.

- Learn which code edition applies, given the 2014 list in the manual and the 2018 edition on the department page.

- Decide who puts the permit question to the office for a re-roof, and who then keeps the paperwork.

- Look at whether any single bid crosses the manual's $25,000 line, or whether a roofing subcontractor's share crosses the Board's line by itself.

- Keep your insurer's written estimate, if you have one, where every bidder can read it.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to be home when my contractor or insurer inspects the roof?

For the exterior portion, usually not — but being present means you see the documented damage yourself and can ask questions of whoever inspects the roof.

Should I repair or replace after partial-slope damage?

Shingle availability and the age of the roof both come into it; your insurer can tell you how your policy addresses matching. Written bids that price both paths let you compare the two side by side.
