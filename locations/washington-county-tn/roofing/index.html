Roofing issues to look for after a storm

  • Partial-slope damage — storms can damage one or two elevations, raising repair-versus-replace questions that comparing bids can help you think through.
  • Hail bruising and granule loss — impact marks that shorten shingle life even when no leak appears immediately.
  • Decking and underlayment issues — discovered only at tear-off, and can be a source of change orders worth understanding in advance.
  • Flashing and penetration damage — check chimneys, vents, and valleys, where post-storm leaks can start.
  • Slow leaks after a storm — minor damage can go unnoticed until water stains show indoors.

What to expect locally

Homeowners in Washington County navigating a storm claim juggle three parallel tracks: the insurance process (adjuster inspection, scope, settlement), the contractor process (bids, scheduling, materials), and their own documentation. Keeping those tracks separate is the single most useful habit — your insurer decides coverage under your policy; your contractor proposes the repair scope; and written bids let you compare scope and pricing side by side.

Be appropriately skeptical of anyone who shows up unsolicited after a storm, pressures you to sign paperwork on the spot, or quotes a price without getting on the roof or examining the damage up close. Storm-chasing crews can show up after any major storm, and one practical defense is unhurried, written bids that you can check against each other.

Season and timing

Post-storm weeks can be congested, and quotes gathered in a hurry may be harder to compare; the weeks after the rush can be a calmer time to review bids. Whatever the calendar says, the sequence stays the same: document first, understand your policy second, compare any written bids you receive third — and take your time rather than rushing to agree with the first person who knocks on the door.

Washington County, Tennessee: storm history beside Johnson City and Jonesborough

The hail sample is small. In NOAA's Storm Events files for 2016 through 2026, the county name WASHINGTON in Tennessee returns 15 hail reports on 9 dates, from July 19, 2016 through June 29, 2026. Reports by year: 2016 (2), 2019 (1), 2020 (1), 2021 (4), 2022 (3), 2023 (2), 2024 (1), 2026 (1); none for 2017, 2018 or 2025. Every report is an inch or bigger: seven at 1.00, four at 1.25, two at 1.50, two at 1.75. One 1.75-inch report is labeled Gray and stamped 6:31 p.m. EST on May 19, 2022, the same evening as 1.50 inches labeled Kincheloe Mill and 1.25 inches labeled Spurgeon. No hail row tops 1.75 inches. The other 1.75-inch report, July 19, 2016, is labeled Jonesboro, yet its narrative places hail the size of a golf ball at Sulphur Springs; labels point near a place without being the place. March 27, 2021 produced three reports: 1.25 and 1.50 inches labeled Mc Kinley, and 1.00 labeled Fairview. June 4, 2023 produced two, both labeled Gray, at 1.00 and 1.25. The calendar splits as March 3, May 5 (three dates), June 4 (three dates), July 2, August 1, so twelve of the fifteen come between March and June.

Thunderstorm wind totals 36 reports falling on 25 dates, with July 8, 2016 first and December 19, 2025 last; each of the 36 is an estimate, since none was taken from an instrument. Pairs of reports and dates for 2016 through 2025 are (6, 3), (1, 1), (4, 3), (4, 2), (3, 3), (2, 1), (2, 2), (9, 5), (2, 2), (3, 3). May leads with 11 reports on 7 dates; August is next at 9 on 5, then July at 5 on 4, and then June, March and September at 3 each (on 3, 2 and 2 dates), with November and December at 1 each. January, February, April and October have none. May 18, 2019 and May 16, 2023 are the busiest, at three reports apiece; New Victory supplies two of the 2023 trio and Mayday the third. Hail and wind reports coincide on one date only, May 18, 2019.

The two top estimates both belong to the night of August 14, 2023: 74 knots at Stewart Hill and 70 knots at Woodlawn. Their stamps, 11:10 p.m. and 11:15 p.m. EST, convert, on a daylight-saving clock, to 12:10 a.m. and 12:15 a.m. on August 15, so a wall clock reads one calendar day later than the file. Stewart Hill's narrative names a small barn with bent metal covering as the only structural harm and says "The divergent damage pattern is consistent with damaging thunderstorm winds." Woodlawn reads "Downburst severe winds from a thunderstorm produced a divergent pattern in the woods, felling several hardwood trees along with broken branches." Two 61-knot estimates come after them, July 8, 2016 (Johnson City) and March 29, 2020 (Sulphur Spgs, its narrative has a tree down across a road in the Sulphur Springs area). A July 29, 2023 report labeled Johnson City mentions many trees and power lines toppled by thunderstorm gusts.

High Wind adds five reports of its own under the county name: February 24, 2016 (estimated 60 knots), October 23, 2017 (estimated 50), January 12, 2024 (a measured 54, from a mesonet station), September 27, 2024 (a measured 52, tagged Helene, narrative "multiple large trees were felled by high winds throughout the county, including all over Johnson City.") and April 2, 2025 (an estimated 52). On tornadoes, no Washington County entry exists for 2016 through 2026.

Two cautions on method. NOAA's format guide describes times as "usually in LST", and every Washington row carries the EST stamp, which runs an hour behind daylight time. Then labels versus places: the Census geocoder puts the coordinates of all 51 hail and thunderstorm wind rows inside Washington County, but the nearest named spot in any row is a label and nothing more. The June 29, 2026 hail report comes from the file NOAA compiled in September 2026, which gets refreshed monthly.

How Morristown and the county EMA describe storms

Weather.gov lists the Morristown office of the National Weather Service as the forecast office for Washington County. That office's guide stresses that lightning frequency is no criterion for a Severe Thunderstorm Warning, and for tornadoes it says a warning goes out when "a tornado is detected on radar or a reliable report of a tornado is received." Both 2023 estimates from August 14 exceed the "58 mph (50kts)" line on the office's criteria page.

The Washington County/Johnson City EMA, reachable at 423-434-6082, introduces itself as "here to help you and your family prepare for emergency situations" and illustrates the point with "A winter storm could confine your family at home." A 2020 hazard mitigation plan is linked on that page.

The permit counters

Which one applies depends on the address. The Building Division of Johnson City, at 601 E. Main Street (423-434-6047), says it accepts and reviews plans for every phase of construction handled at the city level and that its coverage extends to renovations, repairs and additions as well as new development. Its FAQ says that once review is complete, the permit can be obtained only by a contractor holding a license or by an owner living in a single-family house. On inspections the FAQ is blunt: "Currently all inspection requests must be called in." It then sets a 4:30 p.m. dividing line: "requests for inspections received by 4:30 p.m. may be scheduled as early as the next business day, while those received after 4:30 p.m. will be scheduled no earlier than the second business day." No city page opened for this report mentions roofing; ask the city's building staff about a re-roof.

Jonesborough's Building Inspector (423-753-1035) lists a model-code rule covering building, enlarging, altering, repairing, moving or demolishing a structure, or changing how it is occupied: the owner or the owner’s agent must "first make application to the building official and obtain the required permit." The page also lists "Ensure compliance with Historic Zoning Regulations", a question worth asking if your house is downtown. Jonesborough's Work Exempt Form, an excerpt of the 2018 International Residential Code, does not list roofing among the work exempt from permit.

For addresses outside both towns, the Washington County FAQ answers "When are permits required?" with "When constructing any building structure, pool, decks, additions, renovations, manufactured housing and grading." Roofing is not named, so ask the Washington County Zoning Office and Codes Compliance. Its residential application, revised August 20, 2026, prints the inspection number (423) 753-1753 beneath the rule "ALL INSPECTIONS MUST BE SCHEDULED BY 3:00 P.M. THE DAY PRIOR". The form also warns that skipping the permit "may result in your paying double for your permit", with fines of up to $500.00 a day, and it sets a 180-day clock for starting work, after which the permit becomes invalid.

Two Board pages cover the state side. On the contractor page, the Tennessee Board for Licensing Contractors requires a license for projects of $25,000 or more when acting as a prime contractor, and defines the term: "A prime contractor is one who bids or contracts directly with the owner." Over on Home Improvement, the Board's definition of home improvement includes "fences, roofing, painting, and other improvements", but the license requirement there operates only in counties that adopted the law, nine of them, and Washington is not one. The Verify a License link on the Board's pages leads to a search titled "Verify Public Search", which runs on scripts and was not used here.

Before you ask for Washington County bids

- Decide whether the house sits inside Johnson City, inside Jonesborough, or out in the county; each keeps its own permit counter and its own way of booking inspections.

- For a Johnson City address, plan to phone the Building Division early in the day, given the 4:30 p.m. dividing line in its FAQ.

- If the address is out in the county, plan around the 3:00 p.m. cutoff on the day before any inspection.

- If the house is downtown in Jonesborough, ask what the town's historic zoning rules require.

- Find out who will apply for the permit, since starting without one carries the double-fee warning printed on the form for county addresses.

- Keep a copy of your insurer's written estimate, if you have one, that each bidder can read.
