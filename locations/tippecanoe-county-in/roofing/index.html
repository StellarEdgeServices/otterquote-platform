The central Indiana climate and your roofing

In central Indiana, where Tippecanoe County is located, NOAA's 2022 Indiana State Climate Summary notes that the state has a long and deadly history of tornadic storms. It identifies June 2, 1990 as Indiana's largest tornado outbreak, with 37 tornadoes, several of them at F4 intensity, and says spring and summer precipitation has generally been above average since the 1990s.

Roofing issues to look for after a storm

  • Hail bruising and granule loss — impact marks that shorten shingle life even when no leak appears immediately.
  • Slow leaks after a storm — minor damage can go unnoticed until water stains show indoors.
  • Flashing and penetration damage — check chimneys, vents, and valleys, where post-storm leaks can start.
  • Ventilation and code items — older roofs may need code-related upgrades; ask your contractor and your building department how local code items are handled.
  • Decking and underlayment issues — discovered only at tear-off, and can be a source of change orders worth understanding in advance.

What to expect locally

After a storm in Tippecanoe County, the useful work is slow: getting damage documented, raising questions with your insurer, and having the repair scoped carefully. Many homeowners find it helps to slow the process down at the start — documenting damage before tarps and repairs change the evidence, reading their policy before the first phone call, and getting more than one written bid before signing anything.

Local demand can also move in waves. After a widely publicized hail event, contractors across a region may get busy at once. If you receive more than one written bid, comparing them gives you a way to check pricing when demand spikes, and can surface scope differences — what one bidder saw that another missed — before the work starts rather than after.

The county's hail history is lopsided: 2017 alone supplies 12 rows over 4 dates, 2019 supplies 4 over 3 dates, 2026 supplies 2 over 2, and each of 2018, 2020, 2023 and 2024 contributes one. The files hold no Tippecanoe hail rows for 2016, 2021, 2022 or 2025. Typed diameters, in inches, run 0.75 (6 rows), 0.88 (3), 1.00 (8), 1.50 (1), 1.75 (3) and 4.00 (1), so 13 rows over 8 dates reach an inch or larger.

That 4.00 stands alone. Montmorencie, May 27, 2019, filed from a public report with the narrative field empty, so nothing explains how the stone was sized. Tippecanoe's weather zone and the roof table in the state code

Zone INC157 is Tippecanoe's code in Weather Service records, and its forecasts come from the IND office in Indianapolis. Indiana's amended copy of Table R301.2(1) is printed at the end of a guidance sheet from the state's homeland security department about drip edge and ice barriers on asphalt-shingled Class 2 homes. For Tippecanoe its row reads 115 mph wind, 20 psf snow load on the ground, 30 inches in the foundation column, seismic category A, and ice shield marked "No." In a "No" county the sheet leaves the layer to whatever the shingle manufacturer's written installation instructions say.

The age and makeup of Tippecanoe County's housing

Census Bureau survey estimates for 2020 to 2024 count 189,071 people and 80,647 dwellings, occupied or not, in Tippecanoe County. Occupied units total 75,808 and vacant ones 4,839 (6.0 percent). Owner households number 40,470 (53.4 percent of occupied) against 35,338 renter households (46.6 percent). Standalone single-family houses make up 47,272 units (58.6 percent), and mobile homes make up 1,997 (2.5 percent). Census gives 1987 as the median construction year (margin of error 2), meaning roughly half the county's units were standing by the end of 1987. Before 1980 came 34,489 units (42.8 percent); 1980 through 1999 added 19,179 (23.8 percent); 26,979 (33.5 percent) arrived in 2000 or later. The 2000s lead the decades with 14,224 units (17.6 percent), while 8,771 (10.9 percent) are from 1939 or earlier. All of this is sampled, each estimate carries a margin of error, and nothing in these tables says when a particular roof went on.

Which Tippecanoe County office handles your address

Permit counters differ by address. The Tippecanoe County Building Commission, 20 North 3rd Street in Lafayette, 765-423-9225, keeps weekday hours of 8:00 AM to 4:30 PM and lists a general building permit application among its downloads. Its FAQ answers "Where do I get a building permit?" by sending Lafayette addresses to 765-807-1050 and West Lafayette addresses to 765-775-5130, then adds "If you live outside any of these areas, you would be considered to be in the county." Roofing does not appear in that FAQ.

Lafayette's permit desk is part of Engineering and Public Works at 20 N 6th Street, which lists 8 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. Its residential checklist states "The General Contractor will need a $5000 Bond" and requires city licensing for an electrician; the checklist and the permit page leave roofing unmentioned. West Lafayette's Margerum City Hall, 222 N Chauncey Ave, (765) 775-5130, open weekdays 8:00am to 4:30pm, runs a building permit FAQ with the entry "Do I need a reroofing permit?" and the answer "No, not unless any structural materials are being replaced." Battle Ground, Dayton and the other towns were not checked, and no countywide reroofing rule turned up.

Roofer licensing as the state describes it

Roofing is absent from the Professional Licensing Agency's profession list, which names home inspectors, plumbing and real estate among others. Indiana's Attorney General, writing about storm-chaser scams, advises asking your own building department whether a permit applies and says no contractor should begin before one is issued; licensing rules, the leaflet adds, change from one community to another. In Tippecanoe County that leaves the question with whichever office covers your street: Lafayette, West Lafayette, a town or the county.

Tippecanoe County bid checklist

- Pin down the office with authority over your address first: Lafayette, West Lafayette, another town, or the county Building Commission.

- Have each bidder say in writing whether the job needs a permit at your address, and how that answer changes if decking or other structural wood has to be replaced.

- Ask who files for the permit and who requests the inspection.

- The state table leaves Tippecanoe at "No" for ice shield, so have each bidder show you what the shingle brand's own installation sheet says about that layer.

- If a bidder is a general contractor, ask what bond or registration the office covering your address requires; Lafayette's checklist names a $5000 bond.

- Hand every bidder the same copy of any written estimate you hold from your insurer.
