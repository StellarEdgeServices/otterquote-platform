A severe storm in Davidson County can leave roofs with the full menu of problems: hail bruising, wind-lifted shingles, damaged flashing, and the slow leaks that follow. Getting more than one written bid is one way to understand what a repair may cost.

The Middle Tennessee climate and your roofing

NWS Nashville's tornado climatology of Middle Tennessee for 1830 to 2003 says nearly two-thirds of its tornadoes occurred in March, April and May. It reports that the most common hour of the day is 5:00 to 5:59 PM Central Standard Time. For the same 1830 to 2003 record it notes a secondary peak centered around November, when thirty tornadoes, 6 percent of the total, struck, a note that applies to Davidson County's region.

Roofing issues to look for after a storm

  • Slow leaks after a storm — minor damage can go unnoticed until water stains show indoors.
  • Hail bruising and granule loss — impact marks that shorten shingle life even when no leak appears immediately.
  • Wind-lifted and creased shingles — broken seal strips let later storms drive rain under the roof surface.
  • Ventilation and code items — older roofs may need code-related upgrades; ask your contractor and your building department how local code items are handled.
  • Impact damage that hides from the ground — hail strikes are hard to see without getting on the roof, which is why documentation matters.

What to expect locally

After a storm in Davidson County, the useful work is slow: getting damage documented, raising questions with your insurer, and having the repair scoped carefully. Many homeowners find it helps to slow the process down at the start — documenting damage before tarps and repairs change the evidence, reading their policy before the first phone call, and getting more than one written bid before signing anything.

Local demand can also move in waves. After a widely publicized hail event, contractors across a region may get busy at once. If you receive more than one written bid, comparing them gives you a way to check pricing when demand spikes, and can surface scope differences — what one bidder saw that another missed — before the work starts rather than after.

Season and timing

Post-storm weeks can be congested, and quotes gathered in a hurry may be harder to compare; the weeks after the rush can be a calmer time to review bids. Whatever the calendar says, the sequence stays the same: document first, understand your policy second, compare any written bids you receive third — and take your time rather than rushing to agree with the first person who knocks on the door.

The biggest stones come from May 10, 2016: a 2.50-inch report whose narrative describes tennis-ball-size hail along Andrew Jackson Parkway in Hermitage, alongside two 1.75-inch NOAA reports from Old Hickory and Tulip Grove that same night. Reports of 1.50 inches are dated March 25, 2021 (filed under Oak Hill, with narratives naming Green Hills and the corner of Brightwood Avenue and Lombardy Avenue), January 12, 2023 (near Oak Hill) and March 31, 2023 (Bull Run, in a narrative about a storm east of Ashland City). Sorted by month, 54 of those 69 NOAA reports and 23 of the 32 days land between March and June. May alone holds 25 reports on 8 days; July holds one.

Wind fills the file faster. NOAA lists 431 thunderstorm wind reports on 126 days between April 2016 and April 2026, and 107 of those days fall from March through August. June (90 reports) and July (87) are the two heaviest months. The busiest single date in that NOAA record is July 1, 2023 with 25 reports, followed by July 8, 2016 with 20 and June 15, 2016 with 17. The top estimated gust in the NOAA file, 74 knots, appears twice. One is March 25, 2021, filed under Nashville and described as a microburst 6 miles long and half a mile wide, running from the Napier area through East Nashville and Inglewood. The other is December 11, 2021, filed under Maplewood, where the narrative gives a straight-line wind swath about 2 miles long in Madison between Briley Parkway at Gallatin Pike and Neelys Bend Road.

Tornado entries arrive in clusters. The Bordeaux (August 31, 2017), Joelton (November 18, 2017) and Forest Grove (April 28, 2026) entries are rated EF1. The December 11, 2021 narratives name paths near Cub Creek Road, on Martingale Drive in Old Hickory, near Cooks Landing Court and Stewarts Ferry Pike, and near the Stones River at I-40 in Hermitage; no rating is given for them here because NOAA's rating field and narrative text differ on several of those entries. NOAA's 2026 data is preliminary. These entries describe weather across Davidson County. None is a record of a particular home, and none forecasts a future storm.

Housing stock

The Census Bureau's 2020 to 2024 American Community Survey (5-year table DP04) counts 357,113 housing units in Davidson County. Of the 324,907 occupied units, 171,526 (52.8 percent) are owner-occupied and 153,381 are renter-occupied. The same table sorts all units by building type: 176,279 (49.4 percent) are 1-unit detached, 32,704 are 1-unit attached, and 68,742 (19.2 percent) sit in buildings of 20 or more units. By year built, the survey's median is 1986. The 2020 to 2024 survey puts 21,631 units at 1939 or earlier and 76,368 (21.4 percent) at 2010 or later. Adding every band through 1979 in that survey gives 146,507 units, or 41.0 percent. As a general observation, an older home may already have had its first replacement.

Permits and local rules

Permits come from the Metro Nashville Department of Codes and Building Safety, whose pages give 615-862-6500 and a counter at 800 President Ronald Reagan Way, 1st Floor, open Monday through Friday from 7:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Its Residential Building Permits page, updated October 31, 2025, lists "Install siding, roofing, fireplaces or solar panels" among the times a building permit is required, and it describes "repairs to an existing roof not to exceed 33 percent of the roof area" as normal maintenance repairs. The department's renovation page, updated January 17, 2025, has a table headed "Required for 1 and 2 Dwellings." There, "Roofing, New Layer or Replacing More Than 1/3 of The Roof" is marked Yes. So is a carpentry row that includes "Roof Decking More Than 64 Sq. Ft.," while "Gutters, Downspouts" is marked No. Confirm with the department whether your own re-roof needs a permit before work starts.

On that renovation page, the applicant sends a Residential Permit Application to the Zoning Help Desk and lists the work in its Scope of Work section. After zoning review the applicant gets a checklist of other signoffs, and the page says "It is your responsibility to follow the requirements of the checklist." It adds that it is "the applicant's responsibility to schedule the necessary inspections with Metro Nashville at the proper time in the building process."

An owner who occupies or intends to occupy a single-family residence may take out a "Self Permit," with notarized affidavits, and then "the property owner assumes all responsibility" for meeting the codes. If a house is held in an LLC or trust, the renovation page limits a self-permit to work under $25,000. The same page tells owners to check Parcel Viewer for overlays, and for a property in the Neighborhood Conservation Overlay or the Historic Preservation Overlay to "contact the Historic Commission to inquire about any relevant regulations." The pages we read publish no rule on roof layers or ice barrier, so check with the department.

To look up past permits, the Property Permit History page says to open the Parcel Viewer map, search the address and "select Permit History."

On Tennessee Board for Licensing Contractors pages read in early October 2026, Davidson is second in the board's list of nine Home Improvement counties, where the license "is required for residential remodeling projects from $3,000 to $24,999." For work of $25,000 or more, the board's contractor page says a contractor license is needed "before contracting, including bidding, offering to engage, or negotiating a price." The Verify a License search on tn.gov shows a license number.

Before you ask for bids on a Davidson County roof

- Your insurer's written estimate, if one exists, so each bid starts from one document.

- A question for each bidder: who pulls the Metro permit, and will the permit number appear in the contract?

- A question about deck work, since the department's table lists roof decking over 64 square feet as a Yes row.

- A Parcel Viewer check for overlays on your parcel.

- Each bidder's Tennessee license number, checked in the Verify a License search.
