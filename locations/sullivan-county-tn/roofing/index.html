The East Tennessee climate and your roofing

NOAA's Tennessee state climate summary says temperatures decrease as elevation increases, averaging a 3 degree F decline per 1,000 feet. It describes the Cumberland Plateau as averaging 2,000 feet in elevation and Smoky Mountains peaks as reaching 6,000 feet, and it says these higher areas are cooler than lower-elevation areas. These are statewide statements that include Sullivan County.

Roofing issues to look for after a storm

  • Slow leaks after a storm — minor damage can go unnoticed until water stains show indoors.
  • Wind-lifted and creased shingles — broken seal strips let later storms drive rain under the roof surface.
  • Flashing and penetration damage — check chimneys, vents, and valleys, where post-storm leaks can start.
  • Impact damage that hides from the ground — hail strikes are hard to see without getting on the roof, which is why documentation matters.
  • Ventilation and code items — older roofs may need code-related upgrades; ask your contractor and your building department how local code items are handled.

What to expect locally

Homeowners in Sullivan County navigating a storm claim juggle three parallel tracks: the insurance process (adjuster inspection, scope, settlement), the contractor process (bids, scheduling, materials), and their own documentation. Keeping those tracks separate is the single most useful habit — your insurer decides coverage under your policy; your contractor proposes the repair scope; and written bids let you compare scope and pricing side by side.

Ask any contractor for an itemized written estimate, proof of insurance, and local references. The process can take longer after a widespread storm, when repair schedules fill up. Patience and paperwork usually serve homeowners better than speed and pressure.

Season and timing

Storm damage in East Tennessee can arrive in clusters: a single hail event can affect many roofs, gutters, and siding elevations in an area at once. Comparing more than one written bid for the same scope is one way homeowners check pricing when demand spikes.

Sullivan County notes

Sullivan County, Tennessee, in NOAA's storm data

Everything below comes from NOAA's Storm Events bulk files for Tennessee, 2016 through 2026, sorted by the file's own spelling of the county, SULLIVAN. Hail leads off with 12 rows that touch 9 separate calendar days; May 12, 2017 is the first and September 24, 2024 the last. The yearly spread is thin. One row apiece sits in 2017, 2018, 2020, 2021, 2023 and 2024, 2022 contributes six rows on three days, while the years 2016, 2019, 2025 and 2026 show nothing. The smallest stone in the list is an inch across. By size: eight rows at 1.00 inch; one at 1.25 inches (Bridwell Hgts, May 6, 2022); three at 1.50 inches, all dated May 19, 2022 and labeled Lynn Garden, Howard Hill and Long Is. That single May 19 date accounts for four of the twelve rows. Spring dominates: ten rows are logged from March through June, made up of March 2, May 7 over four days and June 1; the two leftovers fall in September.

Treat labels as hints. The hail row of September 24, 2024 carries the label East Kingsport, yet its narrative ends "Both events occurred in Bloomingdale." A NOAA label carries the name of a close-by landmark and nothing like a mailing address. Run through the Census Bureau's geocoder, 67 of Sullivan's 68 rows of hail or thunderstorm wind resolve to points inside Sullivan; the exception is a July 5, 2020 thunderstorm wind row labeled Horse Creek, which resolves to Washington County.

The thunderstorm wind list is longer, 56 rows on 40 days, first on June 4, 2016 and last on December 19, 2025. Six rows are instrument readings; fifty are estimates. For 2016 through 2025, in order, the (rows, days) pairs read (9, 6), (2, 2), (4, 4), (8, 3), (8, 5), (2, 1), (6, 6), (6, 6), (4, 2), (7, 5). July and August have 12 rows apiece, on 10 days and 5 days respectively. September adds 6 rows across 5 days. Combine March, April, May, June, July and August and you reach 38 rows on 27 days; October has none. A single day, August 1, 2019, holds 6 rows.

No thunderstorm wind row exceeds 56 knots. Two estimates sit at that level, both stamped March 29, 2020, one at Howard Hill, the other at Cedar Grove. The firmest numbers are 54-knot instrument readings carried by the airport label "(TRI)TRI CITY ARPT B", logged July 8, 2016 and again January 12, 2023; the 2023 narrative is a single line stating that a gust measured at the Tri-Cities Airport was a thunderstorm wind. On August 16, 2024 the same label carries a 51-knot row whose narrative says "The ASOS weather station at Tri-Cities Regional Airport recorded a 59 mph gust during a severe thunderstorm." Two more narratives describe objects, not houses: a March 27, 2021 row labeled Mill Pt says "A large barn was destroyed at the end of Silvers Chapel Road." and a 55-knot estimate dated July 20, 2018 and labeled Springdale reports traffic signals and wiring down in Kingsport.

A separate NOAA category, High Wind, holds strong wind that no thunderstorm produced. Three such rows exist under the county name: April 1, 2023, estimated at 52 knots; September 27, 2024, estimated at 70 knots, with a narrative that treats it as a severe, prolonged event from Helene across Sullivan County; and December 31, 2024, measured at 50 knots, where the narrative cites a 58 mph gust recorded by the airport ASOS at KTRI. The file records no tornado for Sullivan in the window.

On the clock: NOAA's format guide says times are "usually in LST", and each Sullivan row used here carries EST, a fixed offset that never follows daylight saving. One date shifts as a result: the thunderstorm wind row stamped 11:40 p.m. EST on August 14, 2022 (Holston Vly) was 12:40 a.m. of August 15 by daylight-time reckoning. The 2026 file in use was compiled in September 2026 and gets refreshed every month; it has no Sullivan hail, thunderstorm wind, tornado or High Wind row. Read all of this as county-wide history, which cannot describe one address or a storm still to come.

Warnings, as the Morristown office frames them

Weather.gov lists the Morristown, Tennessee forecast office as the one responsible for Sullivan, along with Blount and Washington. In its Severe Weather Guide that office separates a watch from a warning: "Warnings are much smaller than watches and specific to an individual storm or line of storms." It gives a time frame as well: "The typical duration for a warning is usually between 30 minutes to an hour." On the office's criteria page the wind trigger for a Severe Thunderstorm Warning is "58 mph (50kts)". Against that figure, the 54-knot airport readings sit above 50 knots and the two 56-knot figures farther above it.

Emergency Management for Sullivan County is at 1651 Blountville Blvd, Suite 103, Blountville (423-323-6912), open "8am to 5pm Monday - Friday except designated holidays." A link on its page reads "Click to continue to National Weather Service in Morristown TN." The page also says the county built its Hazard Mitigation Plan "to better address potential natural hazards before they occur", through a "multi-jurisdictional planning process" that involved Bluff City, Bristol and Kingsport.

Housing figures from the Census

The American Community Survey's 2020 to 2024 five-year release, table DP04, tallies 76,324 housing units in Sullivan County. People occupy 68,907 of them; 7,417 stand empty, which is 9.7 percent. Of the occupied stock, 49,708 units are owner-held (72.1 percent) and 19,199 renter-held (27.9 percent). One-unit detached houses make up 53,596 units (70.2 percent of all housing); mobile homes account for 7,937 (10.4 percent); large buildings of 20 or more units, 2,233 (2.9 percent).

For age, B25035 reports 1977 as the median build year, give or take a year. DP04's decade rows put 41,982 units (55.0 percent) into the pre-1980 group, which breaks down to 12,021 built in the 1970s (15.7 percent) and 5,711 built in 1939 or earlier (7.5 percent); another 13,574 (17.8 percent) date from the year 2000 onward. No county-level count reveals the last replacement date of any one roof; closing papers and old invoices do.

Permits and licensing

Planning and Codes, the county's permit office, sits at 3425 Highway 126, Suite 101, Blountville (423-323-6440), on the same posted schedule. Its page reads "Building Permits are required in Sullivan County for all types of residential housing including" a list that ends "decks, and roofs as well as interior remodeling jobs." It also records that county commissioners "adopted the 2018 International Residential Code and the 2018 Energy Code". On the permit application, contractors fill in a line labeled "State License #:", and the county's roster of required inspections includes "Roof tear off inspection required before reroofing". Other lines in the county paperwork govern timing. The permit, "IF NOT EXERCISED, EXPIRES 180 DAYS FROM ISSUANCE AND/OR BETWEEN INSPECTIONS", and applicants are told to "allow for 3 business day turn around for all inspections as your request will be inspected in the order in which it was received." A notice on the inspection list adds that "property owners should not and may not rely upon Sullivan County inspections", so treat the county's inspection as separate from, and not a replacement for, your own review of the finished work.

Inside Kingsport the counter is different. The Building Division (415 Broad Street, 423-229-9393) lists "Roofs: including reroofing and adding roofs over existing decks or patios" among the projects that need permits, in its FAQ. On the question of who may buy a permit, the FAQ says the owner-occupant may buy permits for work he or she does personally, that a hired contractor must buy them, and that a Homeowner’s Affidavit has to be completed before an owner can. The city's contractor page includes the "2024 International Residential Code" in the roster of codes its Building Department enforces, a newer edition than the county's, and adds "Any project over $25,000 requires a Tennessee State Contractor’s License." Bristol's building office was not opened for this page; for a Bristol address, ask the Bristol, Tennessee building department itself.

Two Board pages cover the state side. The contractor page says a license is required for projects of $25,000 or more "when acting as a prime contractor, certain subcontractor, or construction manager/consultant". The two texts differ on the dollar line: the Board says "or more", Kingsport says "over". The Board's second page, for Home Improvement (consulted in October 2026), gives the lower tier as "residential remodeling projects from $3,000 to $24,999", but it switches on only in nine named counties, and Sullivan does not make that list. The Verify a License link on the Board's pages opens a search titled "Verify Public Search". It runs on scripts, so no lookup was run here and that step belongs to you.

Questions for the Sullivan County roof job

- Pick out your counter first: Kingsport's Broad Street office, Bristol, or Planning and Codes in Blountville. Each has its own forms and inspection calls.

- Ask which residential code edition your counter lists, since Kingsport names the 2024 book while the county works from the 2018 one.

- Decide who phones in the roof tear-off check and who stands by for it; the county takes inspection requests in arrival order.

- Check whether a contract total lands on exactly $25,000, the one figure where "over" and "or more" read differently.

- Copy down the number each bidder puts on the "State License #:" line of Sullivan's permit form, then look it up through the Verify a License link on the Board's pages.

- Keep a copy of your insurer's written estimate, if you have one, for every bidder, so every bid is built from one document.
