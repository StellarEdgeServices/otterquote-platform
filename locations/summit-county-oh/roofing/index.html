Roofing issues to look for after a storm

  • Flashing and penetration damage — check chimneys, vents, and valleys, where post-storm leaks can start.
  • Decking and underlayment issues — discovered only at tear-off, and can be a source of change orders worth understanding in advance.
  • Wind-lifted and creased shingles — broken seal strips let later storms drive rain under the roof surface.
  • Ventilation and code items — older roofs may need code-related upgrades; ask your contractor and your building department how local code items are handled.
  • Impact damage that hides from the ground — hail strikes are hard to see without getting on the roof, which is why documentation matters.

What to expect locally

The practical sequence for Summit County homeowners after storm damage: document everything with photos before any cleanup, review your policy and ask your insurer how to report damage, and gather written repair bids so you can compare scope and pricing side by side. Nothing in that sequence requires committing to a contractor early — you can keep your options open while you review any written bids you receive.

Be appropriately skeptical of anyone who shows up unsolicited after a storm, pressures you to sign paperwork on the spot, or quotes a price without getting on the roof or examining the damage up close. Storm-chasing crews can show up after any major storm, and one practical defense is unhurried, written bids that you can check against each other.

Season and timing

Storm damage in northeast Ohio can arrive in clusters: a single hail event can affect many roofs, gutters, and siding elevations in an area at once. Comparing more than one written bid for the same scope is one way homeowners check pricing when demand spikes.

Storm record for Summit County

Pull every hail row that NOAA's Storm Events Database files under Summit County and you are left with 43 entries spread over 20 calendar dates, starting June 16, 2016 and ending June 10, 2026. Dates follow NOAA's records, which are kept in standard time year-round. Thirty of those rows, on 11 dates, describe stones at least an inch across. One date supplies the biggest: on March 28, 2020 NOAA lists 2.50 inches under Akron, 2.25 under Tallmadge, a pair of 2.00-inch rows under Darrowville and Fairlawn, a 1.75-inch Fairlawn row and a 1.50-inch row under Hudson. That is six rows sharing five NOAA place names.

NOAA place names are reference points rather than street addresses, and the narrative text sometimes moves the hail elsewhere. The Darrowville row quotes a trained spotter about 1.7 miles south-southeast of Hudson, while the Akron row describes "tennis ball-size hail about two miles north of downtown Akron." Three other dates hold five rows apiece: May 25, 2019, August 18, 2019 and March 22, 2026. Sorted by calendar year, 2019 leads with 17 hail rows over 6 dates, and 2021, 2022 and 2025 have none. Month by month, March totals 11 rows (2 dates), May 9 (2 dates), August 7 (3 dates) and June 6 (4 dates).

Thunderstorm wind is the larger tally: 134 rows over 53 dates from June 5, 2016 to June 14, 2026. An instrument supplied the gust figure in 12 rows and an estimate supplied it in the other 122. Ranked by row count, August leads with 32 rows on 11 dates, then June with 27 on 9 and April with 23 on 6. April 1, 2023 alone accounts for 10 rows and May 16, 2025 for 9. The highest figure is an 87-knot estimate labeled West Richfield for November 5, 2017, where the narrative speaks of "downburst wind gusts estimated to be as much as 100 mph" over northern Summit County and lists Richfield, Boston, Sagamore Hills and Twinsburg townships. The highest instrument reading, 63 knots on May 25, 2019, sits in a row NOAA labels "(CAK)CANTON ARPT," while its narrative says "This report was at the Akron Fulton Airport."

NOAA files straight-line winds that no thunderstorm produced by forecast zone instead of by county. Summit County's zone carries 10 High Wind rows on 6 dates, plus 4 Strong Wind rows on 3 dates, between December 31, 2018 and January 9, 2024; four of the High Wind rows share March 26, 2021, each an estimated 50 knots.

Five tornado rows land on five different dates between April 15, 2018 and August 6, 2024. The first, an EF1, touched down along Manchester Road in Coventry Township and stayed on the ground, NOAA says, for barely more than a tenth of a mile. The EF0 labeled Comet for April 14, 2019 started over the line in Stark County, and NOAA puts the whole path near 3.6 miles, ending a little south of the Nimisila Reservoir. An EF1 labeled Norton for April 7, 2020 runs 9.81 miles in NOAA's length field: it starts at a ballfield complex on Yonker Street in Barberton, which NOAA's narrative spells Yonkers Road, crosses the Portage Lakes area and stops where Thursby Road meets Discovery Road in Green. On October 21, 2021 a brief EF0 began southwest of OH-91 and Middleton Road in Hudson and ended southeast of the Country Club of Hudson golf course. The most recent, an EF1 labeled Boston Mill on August 6, 2024 with estimated peak winds of 104 mph, goes from Columbia Road in Richfield to the north side of the I-80 Cuyahoga Valley Bridge, just west of Riverview Road in Boston. NOAA built the 2026 file on September 18, 2026 and its newest event is dated June 30; the README next to the files promises a monthly refresh of that year's file, so 2026 totals may move.

Permits and local rules

Akron addresses involve two offices. Akron's Plans and Permits page recalls that "In 2009, the City of Akron Building Department was combined with Summit County Department of Building Standards," and says every project in the city goes to Summit County as well as to the City of Akron. It lists Ohio Residential Code projects among those the county reviews and issues permits for, and zoning among those the city reviews. Neither that page nor the county's Building Standards page names roofing, so raise the roof question with Summit County Building Standards at 330-630-7280, open 7:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. on weekdays at 1030 East Tallmadge Avenue. The county's own hiring tips sheet says "The contractor should be prepared to tell you if your project requires a permit."

Contractor paperwork differs on each side of that split. Akron says a contractor of any trade must register with the city when the work needs a permit; the application asks for an Akron tax registration number and a certificate of liability insurance, and since June 1, 2024 registrations go through the Plan Review and Permits web portal, with renewals taken from June 1 to June 30. Summit County dropped annual registration in March 2024; its page answers the question with "No, not as previously required. Registration is a permit basis only," and adds "No, as of March 2024 a bond is not required." The county's tips sheet asks about state licensing for "plumbing, mechanical or electrical" contractors, and its page links a Contractor License Lookup. The county page also says a homeowner can pull a permit with a notarized affidavit, except for gas lines.

The county page says it provides residential plan review and inspection for 22 jurisdictions in Summit County, and that safety violations are handled by your own city, township or village zoning office, so an address outside Akron may answer to the county, a local office, or both.

What to line up before you ask for bids

- A PDF or paper copy of whatever written estimate your insurer sent, to show each bidder.

- The office that handles a roof permit where you live: Akron Plans and Permits together with Summit County Building Standards, or the building or zoning staff of your own township, city or village.

- If you live in Akron, a question for each bidder about its Akron contractor registration, since the city ties registration to permitted work.
