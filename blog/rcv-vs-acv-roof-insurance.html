ACV vs. RCV Roof Insurance: What They Mean

ACV and RCV are two ways a policy can value a roof. Here is what each term means and where to look on your own policy.

By Otter Quotes · May 29, 2026

← All Articles

When your insurer pays a roof claim, the amount you receive depends heavily on one policy detail most homeowners don’t notice until after a loss: whether your policy settles on an Actual Cash Value (ACV) or Replacement Cost Value (RCV) basis. In the example below, the difference is $7,680. This article explains both calculations in plain language — with illustrative numbers — so you can see how a policy may calculate a payment. Your own policy sets the terms.

1

The Core Difference: Depreciation

The entire ACV vs. RCV distinction comes down to one word: depreciation. Depreciation is the reduction in value that occurs as property ages and wears (Triple-I defines it as the decrease in value due to age, obsolescence, wear and tear and other factors). Your roof was worth more on day one than it is after ten years of sun, wind, and weather.

  • Replacement Cost Value (RCV) settlements commonly ignore depreciation and are based on what it costs to replace your damaged roof with a new one of comparable quality.
  • Actual Cash Value (ACV) settlements commonly apply depreciation: the RCV minus the accumulated depreciation based on your roof’s age and expected lifespan.

Here’s the key insight: the roof physically costs the same amount to replace regardless of which policy you have. The difference is how much the policy contributes versus how much comes out of your pocket.

Where to find your policy type: Open your homeowners insurance declarations page (the one-page summary at the front of your policy). Look for the section on dwelling coverage — it should indicate “Replacement Cost” or “Actual Cash Value.” If your declarations page mentions a separate roof endorsement or a “roof schedule,” read that section carefully — some policies cover the dwelling at RCV but the roof at ACV.
2

Side-by-Side Example: The Same Claim, Two Very Different Payouts

Let’s work through a concrete example. Assume a hail storm damages a 12-year-old asphalt shingle roof. The contractor’s estimate to replace it is $16,000. The roof’s expected lifespan is 25 years. Your deductible is $1,500. The figures are illustrative arithmetic only; your policy and your insurer’s estimate set the actual amounts.

ACV Policy RCV Policy
Replacement cost $16,000 $16,000
Roof age 12 years 12 years
Expected lifespan 25 years 25 years
Depreciation % 48% (12 ÷ 25) N/A — not applied
Depreciation amount $7,680 $0
ACV of roof $8,320 $16,000
Less deductible −$1,500 −$1,500
Insurance pays (example) $6,820 $14,500*
You pay out of pocket $9,180 $1,500*

* RCV policies typically issue an initial ACV payment, then release the held-back depreciation once you complete repairs. See Section 3.

The same damage, the same contractor, the same $16,000 job — but in this example the ACV homeowner would pay $9,180 out of pocket while the RCV homeowner would pay only the $1,500 deductible. That’s a $7,680 difference.

3

How RCV Actually Pays: The Two-Check System

If you have an RCV policy, you may be surprised when the first insurance check arrives and it’s less than the contractor’s estimate. This is common. Many RCV policies use a two-payment structure (North Carolina Department of Insurance explains that, depending on the policy, a company may first pay actual cash value and later pay the difference once repairs are done and receipts are submitted; your policy sets the terms):

  • First payment (ACV): Your insurer commonly pays the depreciated value of the roof upfront — the same amount an ACV policy would pay. In the example above, that would be $6,820. This is sometimes called the “initial payment” or “ACV payment.”
  • Second payment (recoverable depreciation): After you complete the repairs and submit documentation (contractor invoice, photos of completed work), the insurer commonly releases the held-back depreciation — $7,680 in our example, which would bring the total to the full replacement cost. Your policy sets the documentation and timing requirements.
The catch: Policies commonly require you to complete the repairs before the second payment is released. If you keep the first check without repairing the roof, the held-back depreciation may not be paid. And some policies set a deadline, measured from the date of loss, by which repairs must be completed and documented; check yours.

4

When ACV Policies Make Sense (and When They Don’t)

ACV policies typically have lower premiums than RCV policies. For some homeowners, that trade-off makes sense. For others, it’s a significant financial risk. Here’s how to think about it:

  • ACV may make sense if your roof is brand-new or nearly new (depreciation is minimal), you have significant savings to cover out-of-pocket costs, or the premium savings are substantial and you’re willing to self-insure the depreciation gap.
  • ACV is risky if your roof is 10+ years old (depreciation adds up fast), you don’t have substantial savings available for an unexpected repair, or your roof is your biggest potential insurance claim exposure.
  • Watch for “ACV roof endorsements” on RCV policies. Some insurers sell RCV policies but add an endorsement that limits the roof specifically to ACV. In that case your walls, ceilings, and structure may be settled at replacement cost while your roof — the most weather-exposed part of your home — is settled at ACV. This hybrid approach is common in high-wind and high-hail markets. Always check for roof-specific language in your endorsements.
5

How Depreciation Is Calculated

Insurance adjusters commonly use depreciation tables (sometimes called “depreciation guides”) to determine how much to deduct. The calculation methodology varies by insurer and state, but the most common approach is straight-line depreciation:

Depreciation % = Age ÷ Expected Useful Life

For a 15-year-old roof on a 25-year expected lifespan: 15 ÷ 25 = 60% depreciated. In this example, a $15,000 replacement would be valued at $6,000 ACV.

  • Expected useful life varies by material. Standard asphalt shingles: 20–25 years. Architectural/dimensional shingles: 25–30 years. Metal roofing: 40–70 years. Wood shake: 25–30 years. Tile: 40–50 years. Adjusters commonly use baselines like these to calculate depreciation.
  • Condition matters too. A well-maintained 15-year-old roof may receive a more favorable depreciation assessment than a neglected 10-year-old roof. Adjusters commonly factor in observable condition.
  • Ask how depreciation was calculated. If you believe the depreciation assessment is too aggressive, ask your insurer how to raise the question and what your policy provides. Documentation showing the roof was recently maintained, inspected, or partially replaced may be relevant.
Non-recoverable vs. recoverable depreciation: Under some RCV policies, not all depreciation is recoverable. Some items — like old flashing, worn pipe boots, or obsolete vents — may be depreciated at a non-recoverable rate. Ask your insurer for its depreciation worksheet, which commonly breaks this out. Items marked “non-recoverable” are commonly not included in the second payment; your policy sets the terms.
6

What to Do If You Have an ACV Policy

If you discover you’re on an ACV policy, you have options:

  • Ask your carrier to upgrade to RCV. Call your agent and ask whether your roof coverage can be switched to RCV and what it would cost. Some carriers limit coverage changes on roofs older than a certain age, so it helps to ask before the next storm.
  • Get competitive bids before accepting a settlement. On an ACV claim, the depreciation amount is set by the insurer’s estimate, while contractor pricing varies. Getting multiple bids may surface a contractor whose price fits within the ACV payment, which could reduce your out-of-pocket exposure.
  • Document your roof’s condition proactively. If you maintain your roof well, get an annual inspection with a written report. This documentation can help support your position if depreciation is questioned when a claim occurs.
  • Consider the deductible and out-of-pocket math before filing small claims. On an ACV policy with a heavily depreciated roof, your insurance payment on a small repair may not exceed your deductible. Filing a claim that ends in no payment — while generating a claims record that could affect future premiums — may not be worth it. Talk to your agent about the cost-benefit.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if I have ACV or RCV coverage?

Check your insurance declarations page — the summary sheet that comes with your policy. Look for language describing your dwelling coverage: “Replacement Cost” means RCV, “Actual Cash Value” means ACV. Also check for any roof-specific endorsements, which sometimes override the general policy type and apply ACV specifically to the roof even when the rest of the dwelling is on RCV. If you can’t find clear language, call your insurance agent and ask directly: “Is my roof covered at replacement cost or actual cash value?”

Can I switch from ACV to RCV after a storm but before filing a claim?

Ask your agent whether a coverage change made after a storm would apply to that loss. The time to review your coverage is before a loss, ideally at your next renewal period. Call your agent now if you want to ask about changing it.

Why did my insurer send a smaller check than the contractor’s estimate?

On many RCV policies, the initial payment is the ACV amount — the replacement cost minus depreciation. The balance (recoverable depreciation) is commonly held until repairs are complete and documentation is submitted. This two-payment system is common. On an ACV policy, the initial payment is commonly the full settlement, with no second payment for depreciation. Check your policy type and ask your insurer how your claim is being paid.

What is the deadline to collect recoverable depreciation?

Policies set their own deadline for completing and documenting repairs, and the window varies by policy. After a policy’s deadline passes, the insurer may limit payment to the ACV amount, and the recoverable depreciation may no longer be paid. Check your policy’s claim settlement provisions or ask your adjuster for the specific deadline on your claim.

Does having an RCV policy mean I pay nothing out of pocket for a roof replacement?

Not necessarily; policies commonly apply a deductible. In an example where the replacement cost is $16,000 and the deductible is $2,000, the deductible would come out of the settlement, leaving $14,000 as the figure the insurer’s payment is based on (assuming full RCV settlement with no non-recoverable depreciation items). Some items may also be categorized as non-recoverable depreciation, increasing your out-of-pocket cost. Compared with an ACV settlement on an older roof, an RCV settlement is commonly larger. Your policy sets the terms.

What happens if the contractor’s final invoice is less than the insurer’s replacement cost estimate?

Replacement-cost settlements are commonly limited to what you actually spent. In an example where the insurer’s estimate was $16,000 and the contractor’s price was $14,000, the settlement would commonly be based on $14,000 minus your deductible rather than the full $16,000. Property insurance is generally designed to cover an actual loss, not to produce a profit. Insurers commonly ask for your contractor’s invoice when you request the recoverable depreciation payment; ask your insurer what it needs.
